## Nociception: encoding a stimulus as neural activity

Some free nerve endings in skin are nociceptors that respond to mechanical, thermal or chemical stimuli capable of threatening tissue. Receptors and ion channels participate in transduction, followed by electrical activity along afferent fibers. Fiber types differ in sensitivity and conduction speed, and inflammation can alter sensitivity. This encoding process is nociception. Activity in an ending or a sensory neuron alone cannot establish what pain a person experiences.

## Spinal circuits: parallel reflex and ascending routes

Input from the body can reach spinal interneurons and motor neurons, coordinating flexors and related muscle groups in withdrawal. The circuit need not await a conscious decision. Other neurons convey information toward multiple brain relays and networks at the same time. The two drawn routes summarize different roles; they do not imply one literal fork, an absence of synapses, or a universal interval between withdrawal and conscious sensation. Muscles transmit a pull through tendons to bones, and real withdrawal involves joints and several muscle groups. The lower inset shows only shortening bringing an attachment closer; it does not reproduce a whole arm’s anatomical movement.

## Pain: a personal experience, not a damage scale

Pain includes sensory and emotional experience and is influenced by bodily state, attention, memory, expectations and context. Visible wound size alone does not explain pain intensity. Nervous-system disease or persistent changes in sensitivity can also produce or sustain pain without an obvious new injury. Respecting someone’s pain report matters alongside investigating its cause. This page illustrates an acute protective response; brightness does not calculate damage, and the animation cannot diagnose an individual.

## A common misconception

Withdrawing a hand does not establish that pain was already consciously experienced. An absence of visible injury does not establish an absence of pain. Reflexes, nociception and pain are distinct concepts.

## Model limits

Enlarged tissues from different locations are placed together. Routes, cells and synapses are greatly simplified; crossing pathways and descending modulation are omitted. Progress is for observation, not measured latency, diagnosis, a pain score or an injury experiment. Scrubbing backward reviews the illustration. The muscle and linearly moving hand show only the pull. Tendon lengths stay fixed in this inset without modeling their elasticity, and movement direction does not locate the actual stimulus.

### A small message begins

Have you ever touched something and quickly pulled your hand away? Today we will not touch anything hot or hurt ourselves. We will follow a little message on the screen instead. First, zoom into the skin. Fine nerve endings there can respond to some changes that might harm the body. What they send is a nerve signal, not a tiny ball of pain traveling through you.

*Scene cue: Begin with the whole diagram, zoom into the skin endings, then return to the full routes.*

### A place where routes branch

Now let the picture move slowly. Follow the warm-colored dot along the nerve to a place called the spinal cord. Pause and find the two routes that branch here. The green one passes through a circuit in the body and connects with a motor nerve and muscle. The purple one carries information toward the brain. You can emphasize each route in turn. A dimmer route is still working; it has not really switched off.

*Scene cue: Advance to the spinal branch and pause. Emphasize each route while retaining both.*

### The body can protect itself first

Keep watching. The green route reaches the muscle, and the illustrated hand pulls back a little. This protective action is called a reflex. Sometimes the body starts moving before we have had time to decide what to do. Now look at the purple route: information is still traveling onward. Pulling away and clearly feeling pain are related, but they are not exactly the same event. They are not one single button.

*Scene cue: Advance to withdrawal and pause to compare the muscle with the continuing ascending signal.*

### Listen carefully to the body

In the brain, information is processed along with the body’s condition, what we notice and our past experiences. Pain can tell us that we need protection and care, but it is not a ruler for measuring a wound. A small-looking wound can hurt a lot. No visible wound does not mean pain is pretend. If you feel unwell, tell an adult who cares for you. Together, you can listen carefully to what your body is telling you.

*Scene cue: Finish on multiple brain-processing nodes, the full routes and the care message.*
