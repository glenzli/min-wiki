## Grains, boundaries and molecules are different scales

Ordinary ice has an open, ordered hydrogen-bonded molecular structure. Vast numbers of molecules form a grain; differently oriented grains meet at boundaries within a continuous body of ice. Hexagonal symmetry describes crystal structure, so a polycrystalline cup of ice need not resemble a collection of six-sided snowflakes. Clear ice may contain multiple grains. Trapped air bubbles scatter light but are distinct from boundaries, and are not necessary for ice’s lower density. Melting changes order and motion without splitting water into hydrogen and oxygen.

## Temperature differs from phase-change energy

At fixed pressure, energy transfer during a pure substance’s phase change can alter its state without immediately changing temperature. Nucleation, supercooling, dissolved substances and heat transfer affect real freezing. The familiar zero-degree melting point is conditional, and the progress slider is neither a thermometer nor a melting-time prediction.

## Evaporation, boiling and condensation

Evaporation occurs at a surface below boiling; boiling involves growing vapor bubbles within the liquid and depends on pressure. Vapor can condense on a sufficiently cold surface. Gaseous water is invisible; familiar white mist usually consists of tiny droplets. Temperature, humidity, airflow and area all influence evaporation.

## A common misconception

Water vapor is invisible; the visible parts of white mist are usually droplets, not enlarged molecules.

### Water changes its form

An ice cube, a glass of water and invisible vapor in the air look different, but they can all be water. Choose melting and move forward slowly. There is less ice and more liquid water. The water has not become a different substance; it has changed its state.

*Scene cue: Select melting and slowly advance the cup view.*

### Imagine a magnifying glass

The inset helps us imagine extremely tiny water molecules. In ice they have more order; in liquid they remain close but can change positions. Pause, then choose freezing. In the cup, crystal grains grow and meet their neighbors, joining into one layer of ice. Each grain contains enormous numbers of molecules, not just one.

*Scene cue: Choose freezing and compare the thin, partial and full stages; enable grain boundaries, pause three seconds, then point to the molecular inset.*

### Water enters the air

Switch to a separate open cup and follow the gold marker. Some water leaves the surface and enters the air, reducing the water in the cup. This happens without boiling: clothes and wet ground dry this way too. Symbols help us imagine where water goes, but real vapor is invisible.

*Scene cue: Choose the surface-to-lid route, play until before the cold lid appears, and compare the surface with the gold marker.*

### It can return

Keep playing the same cup's story. As the cold lid comes near, some vapor forms small drops beneath it. Not all water that left the cup returns. Water changes state while remaining water. Next time you notice ice, droplets or something drying, name the change you see.

*Scene cue: Continue the same route to the drops beneath the lid, then point to the water left in the cup.*
