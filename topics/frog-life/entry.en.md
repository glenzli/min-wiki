Is growing up only getting bigger? How do tadpole, caterpillar and grasshopper routes differ?

Inspect the frog's limbs and tail, the butterfly's pupa diagram and wing preparation. Compare body, food, breathing and movement side by side. Each animal has an independent timeline, not equal days or sizes.

## One individual develops; two cases keep separate clocks

The frog's continuous morphology and butterfly stage observations address shared questions. In a common frog route, hind limbs become visible before forelimbs emerge, with limbs and tail briefly coexisting. Tail resorption involves regulated cell death, tissue breakdown and reuse of material. A froglet still needs to grow to sexual maturity. Butterfly egg, larva, pupa and adult are also one individual's stages; returning to eggs denotes reproduction and the next generation. Comparison presets align questions, not equal age, body size or developmental duration.

## Body changes connect feeding, breathing and movement

Visible form is only part of development. Frog mouth and digestive structures change, as do the contributions of gills, skin and lungs to gas exchange. Lung function can begin in tadpoles, rather than switching on when limbs are complete. Caterpillars accumulate reserves; pupae remain metabolically active while tissues remodel. Not every tissue dissolves into an unstructured liquid. Insect spiracles and tracheae differ from frog lungs. After emergence, fluid helps wings expand and the wings must harden; visible wings are not necessarily flight-ready.

## Compare routes without making one route universal

Butterfly egg–larva–pupa–adult development is complete metamorphosis in insects. Grasshopper egg–nymph–adult development has no distinct pupa and is incomplete metamorphosis. Nymphs typically resemble adults more closely and develop through repeated moults. These insect categories are not used here to classify frogs. Some frogs develop directly without free-swimming tadpoles. Diet, host plants and timing vary with species and conditions. The third case is a route diagram, and the pupa interior is explanatory—not anatomical measurement, universal timing or a physical movement simulation.

## A common misconception

Frog tails are resorbed rather than dropped, and pupae keep developing rather than simply sleeping. Not all metamorphosis includes a pupa; insect complete/incomplete categories do not classify frogs here.

## Model limits

Enlarged, representative illustrations, not species-specific days, sizes or organ measurements. Some frogs develop directly without free-swimming tadpoles. Butterfly stages revisit one individual's life, not a continuous anatomical reconstruction. Froglets still need to mature; the next generation's eggs result from later reproduction. Observe wild animals in place; do not touch pupae or wings.

### More than getting bigger

Begin with eggs in a pond. A tadpole hatches with a long tail and looks unlike a frog on the bank. On a leaf, a butterfly egg hatches into a caterpillar, not a miniature winged adult. Follow two different animals, each with its own progress, and look for new abilities as their bodies change.

*Scene cue: Visit both larval observations, then return to the frog.*

### New legs, a remaining tail

Move the frog's progress slowly. Find the hind legs, then the forelegs. Pause: four limbs and a tail can exist together! The tail is gradually resorbed and some material reused, rather than falling into the water. The froglet at the end is not yet mature; it still has growing to do.

*Scene cue: Pause with four limbs and a tail, then inspect gradual tail resorption.*

### A pupa is not a long sleep

Select the butterfly's pupa and open the inside diagram. A quiet outside hides continuing development, supported by reserves accumulated as a caterpillar. Select the adult and watch its wings: small and crumpled, then expanded, then gradually hardening. Our playback reveals steps, not real development compressed into an actual few seconds.

*Scene cue: Reveal the pupa diagram, then play and pause independent wing preparation.*

### One question, different answers

Place the two animals side by side and compare food, breathing or movement. They did not start at the same time or grow at the same rate. Look at the grasshopper route too: egg, nymph, adult, with no pupa in between. Animals have different ways of growing. Watch wild animals in place and avoid touching delicate pupae and wings.

*Scene cue: Switch the four comparison lenses and inspect the grasshopper no-pupa route diagram.*
