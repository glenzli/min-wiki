What happens when flowing water meets a rock? Do both sides of a bend change in the same way?

Play and pause. Follow the outlined gold grain from the outer bank into the flow and onto the inside. Inspect the bend without resetting progress; compare the whole route under different rock conditions.

## Water follows a hydraulic gradient

Gravity, pressure differences and boundary resistance govern river flow. Water-surface elevation generally falls downstream, although local eddies can reverse the flow. Rocks, terrain and bed material influence routes. A bend need not start at one rock: small bank differences, floods and prolonged erosion also reshape channels.

## Bends connect erosion and deposition

In many alluvial rivers, outer banks erode while inner banks accumulate sediment. Velocity differences and secondary circulation transport the grains. As the outer bank retreats and the inner bank grows, a bend can migrate. Cohesive soils, roots, discharge and engineered banks can alter this pattern.

## Sediment records river change

Coarse grains can roll, slide or hop near the bed; finer material can remain suspended, and dissolved substances travel with the water. Transport depends on flow, grain size, density and cohesion. Comparing banks, bars and sediment layers over time provides evidence of change beyond a single simplified animation. The displayed grains keep their identities from bank erosion through transport to deposition. Real rivers also receive sediment from elsewhere and carry material farther downstream.

## A common misconception

Rivers do not bend only to avoid rocks. Outer erosion and inner deposition are common patterns, with geological and engineered exceptions.

## Model limits

Channel and grain paths are prescribed teaching diagrams, not three-dimensional flow solutions. The 16 persistent grains represent one transported group, not the entire river sediment budget. Flow, time and concentration are not measurements.

### Follow the water

Imagine watching a stream from a safe bank as it comes down a hill. It does not draw a ruler-straight line; it follows a route downhill through the landscape. Today the stream fits on our screen. Look at the rock ahead, predict which way water will pass, then move forward slowly.

*Scene cue: Play from zero and follow the white water markers downstream rather than jumping to the last frame.*

### Change one condition

Move the rock to the other side and look again. Has the route changed? The landscape influences where water flows. Real rivers have many reasons to bend, though. Softer or harder banks, and more or fewer roots, can help the channel develop differently over a long time.

*Scene cue: Pause and change rock position at the same progress, then continue.*

### Water carries grains

Find the gold sediment dots. Follow the one with a dark outline: first at the bank, then carried by water, then resting along the inside of the bend. Inspect the bend and pause. Can you still find the same grain? It has not disappeared; water moved it from one place to another.

*Scene cue: Inspect the bend around 45%, follow the outlined grain, and advance to 85% to see deposition.*

### A changing map

A river moves material and gradually redraws its own map. A flood and many years of flow can both leave traces. Our short animation compresses that long story. Next time you visit a river with an adult, look from a safe place for bars and bends, and wonder where sediment is leaving and arriving.

*Scene cue: Hold at 100% with settled grains still present, then scrub back to their outer-bank origin.*
