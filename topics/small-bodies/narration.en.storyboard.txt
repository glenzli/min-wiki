01 · Is this still the same rock?
[Move the light back and forth over the retained C-type sample, rotate to the back, then select Ceres. Explain that each model is fitted independently.]

Move the light from one side to the other. One side brightens while the other darkens. It is still the same small body, with the same material. Scientists use features in reflected light, spacecraft and samples as evidence about composition. Rounder Ceres also lives in the main belt. Calling it a dwarf planet involves gravity and orbital conditions, not simply deciding which body is bigger.

02 · Gas and dust come before the tails
[Scrub from aphelion to perihelion, retaining the nucleus while sunward jets, coma and both tails develop. Pause near perihelion.]

Far away, the nucleus quietly follows its path around the Sun. Closer in, exposed ice warms straight into gas, carrying some dust out with it. A fuzzy coma surrounds the nucleus. The Sun influences the released material too. The thin ion tail points generally away from the Sun, while the broad dust tail curves. These are not flames burning in space.

---

03 · Try a surface without exposed ice
[At the same orbital position, switch exposed ice off and on. Scrub to the outgoing side, then back to the same intermediate condition to compare reproducible states.]

Keep the same path and change one surface condition. Without ice available to sublime, this activity produces no coma or tails. Real comets are more complicated: the kinds of ice, what covers the surface and earlier heating can all affect activity. Let the comet move away again and keep relating its tails to the Sun's position.

04 · Where does the dust go?
[Rotate the main and Kuiper belts to see thickness. Compare Earth outside and inside the illustrative stream, then open the separately scaled Oort structure.]

Small members occupy the asteroid belt, Kuiper Belt and space between planets, with plenty of empty space between them. Comets and collisions add dust. When Earth enters a debris stream, some fragments can enter the atmosphere and make a meteor's light. The more distant Oort Cloud is inferred from indirect clues: an explanation of a remote reservoir, rather than a photograph taken from outside.
