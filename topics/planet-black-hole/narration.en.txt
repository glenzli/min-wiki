Today a planet is our traveler: an Earth-like rocky world or a Jupiter-like gas world. In this comparison, they have different mean densities. Start with the more distant route: the distance of the pass changes the strength of the tides. A planet’s own structure matters too, but this small model cannot calculate all the ways rock breaks or gas flows.

Let’s keep the route the same and change only the kind of planet. Pause and predict which might be pulled apart more easily. Then play to the closest point. Have outer layers left? Is a central clump still together? Changing one thing at a time makes the comparison easier to understand.

Keep following the original material. Some stretches into a long tail. Some follows a curved path and returns. Coming back does not mean falling straight in or already forming a disk. Look from above: which paths are more stretched out? Faint lines record the recent paths of a few pieces of material. They are not new solid rings.

Use the close-up to find orange patches. Real material may collide and heat up when it meets other material. The changing brightness helps us imagine this, but it is not a temperature measurement. Material can keep orbiting even as its glow fades. Now try the farther route with the same planet. Does it keep more of its original shape? This comparison helps us understand tides; it is not a forecast of Earth’s future.
