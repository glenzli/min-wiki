How would Earth-like and Jupiter-like planets with different mean densities respond along the same black-hole encounter?

Keep the route fixed and compare tidal scale and deformation for both planets. Then track the tail and returning paths from above, and inspect changing orange patches in the close-up.

## Density sets a tidal scale

In a Newtonian approximation, resistance of a self-gravitating body to tides depends on mean density. An order-of-magnitude radius is rₜ≈R(M/m)^(1/3). Substituting m≈4πρR³/3 gives rₜ proportional to ρ^(−1/3) for the same black hole. Lower-density bodies may be affected farther out. Structure-dependent coefficients are omitted, so this is not a universal material failure threshold.

## Compare orbit and structure separately

The penetration factor β=rₜ/rₚ compares tidal radius with closest approach. Larger β means a relatively deeper encounter. Hold the route fixed when comparing density, then hold planet type fixed when comparing routes. Internal layers, spin, strength, and encounter speed also matter. Deformation is not identical to total destruction; inspect released material, the remaining core, and escaping fragments separately.

## Returning is not disk formation; fading is not capture

Debris has different orbital energies and angular momenta. Some escapes, while some returns on eccentric orbits. Physical interactions and shocks may heat the material, without immediately creating a complete disk. Here, gentle dissipation starts after bound debris turns inward following outward motion. Local orange brightness responds to the approximate energy loss and also decays. Faint lines mark a few samples’ recent paths. Brightness is not a temperature, accretion rate or physical spectrum; rock fracture, gas pressure and vaporization are not calculated.

## A common misconception

Low density does not mean that being lighter makes a body inevitably fall in. Returning does not instantly make a disk. Faint lines show recent paths, and orange patches are not thermometers.

## Model limits

One material population uses weakening support and central gravity to illustrate deformation and release, with simplified dissipation after bound debris returns and eccentric motion retained. The model does not solve rock fracture, gas pressure, vaporization, physical collisions or relativistic fluid dynamics, or predict a known planet’s disruption, disk formation or captured fraction.

### Choose a traveler

Today a planet is our traveler: an Earth-like rocky world or a Jupiter-like gas world. In this comparison, they have different mean densities. Start with the more distant route: the distance of the pass changes the strength of the tides. A planet’s own structure matters too, but this small model cannot calculate all the ways rock breaks or gas flows.

*Scene cue: Begin with the rocky planet on a distant route.*

### Change one thing

Let’s keep the route the same and change only the kind of planet. Pause and predict which might be pulled apart more easily. Then play to the closest point. Have outer layers left? Is a central clump still together? Changing one thing at a time makes the comparison easier to understand.

*Scene cue: Compare both planets on one route; pause four seconds before closest approach.*

### Follow the returning material

Keep following the original material. Some stretches into a long tail. Some follows a curved path and returns. Coming back does not mean falling straight in or already forming a disk. Look from above: which paths are more stretched out? Faint lines record the recent paths of a few pieces of material. They are not new solid rings.

*Scene cue: Use the top view to follow the tail, eccentric returns and recent paths slowly.*

### Inspect the glow, then compare

Use the close-up to find orange patches. Real material may collide and heat up when it meets other material. The changing brightness helps us imagine this, but it is not a temperature measurement. Material can keep orbiting even as its glow fades. Now try the farther route with the same planet. Does it keep more of its original shape? This comparison helps us understand tides; it is not a forecast of Earth’s future.

*Scene cue: Observe orange patches and fading, then hold planet type fixed while comparing near and far routes.*
