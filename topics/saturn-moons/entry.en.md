Do differently sized moons around Saturn take the same time to finish an orbit?

Open Saturn’s moon view in the Solar System explorer and let the orbit illustration run. Select Mimas and Titan in turn to compare period and diameter readouts. Read the discoveries about Titan and Enceladus, distinguishing screen markers, physical sizes and spacecraft evidence.

## Different orbits in one gravitational field

When Saturn dominates and perturbations are neglected, T²≈4π²a³/(GM), so moons farther out generally have longer periods. The seven selected moons share a simulation clock, but orbital spacing is compressed and bodies enlarged. Real orbits include resonances, eccentricity, and inclination changes. Orbit guides are not material rings, and moons are not beads attached to Saturn’s rings.

## Very different satellite environments

Titan has a thick, mainly nitrogen atmosphere and confirmed surface lakes and seas of methane and ethane. Its liquid cycle is not Earth’s water cycle. Enceladus has a global ocean beneath its ice shell and releases water vapor and icy particles near its south pole. Cassini studied these environments using images, spectra, and particle measurements. Water and organic compounds do not establish the presence of life.

## Compare diameter readouts

Selecting a moon shows its orbital period and diameter. Compare the diameter values with their units: bodies and distances in the orbit view are scaled separately, so the displayed spheres are not a common-length measurement. Titan’s physical diameter is much larger than Enceladus’s. These seven are a teaching selection, not Saturn’s total; complete inventories and counts require a source and date.

## A common misconception

Saturn has more than seven moons. Titan’s surface lakes are not liquid water, and Enceladus’s plumes do not prove life has been found.

## Model limits

Circular orbits and illustrative phases simplify motion; body sizes and orbital distances are scaled separately. Moons appear as simplified spheres. Atmospheres, oceans and south-polar plumes are spacecraft discoveries described in text, not close-up surface or plume simulations in this view. There is no common true-scale moon-size workbench; compare the diameter values.

### Partners beyond the rings

Saturn has beautiful rings and many moons nearby. Today we will meet seven of them, but Saturn does not have only seven. Some are large, some small, some nearer, and some farther away. Choose a moon, read its name, and locate it in the picture so you can keep following it.

*Scene cue: Select Titan in the orbit overview.*

### Who completes a lap?

Let the moons orbit and compare Mimas, farther in, with more distant Titan. Pause and predict: over the same playback interval, which makes more laps? Select each one and read its orbital period. The moons share one Saturn, but they do not travel as a matching parade at one speed. Each has its own rhythm.

*Scene cue: Compare Mimas and Titan; pause four seconds.*

### Two unusual worlds

Select Titan and read the spacecraft discoveries. It has a thick atmosphere and lakes of liquid, but that liquid is not the water we drink. Next select Enceladus: it has a bright icy shell and sprays water vapor and icy grains from its southern region. Spacecraft discovered these features with careful pictures and measurements. The spheres help us locate the moons; they do not unfold those landscapes or plumes in the picture.

*Scene cue: Select Titan and Enceladus in turn and read their discoveries; do not switch to a close-up or show simulated plumes.*

### Read their physical sizes

Finally, compare the diameter readouts. Select Titan and note its diameter, then read Enceladus’s diameter. Titan is much larger. To keep the moons visible, the picture adjusts body sizes and distances separately; its spheres and gaps cannot be used as a ruler. These worlds hold many questions to investigate. Even where there is water, we cannot immediately conclude that something is living there.

*Scene cue: Select Titan and Enceladus in turn to compare diameter readouts. Keep the orbit view and explain that spheres and spacing do not share a true length scale.*
