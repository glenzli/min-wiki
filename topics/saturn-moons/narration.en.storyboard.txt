01 · Partners beyond the rings
[Select Titan in the orbit overview.]

Saturn has beautiful rings and many moons nearby. Today we will meet seven of them, but Saturn does not have only seven. Some are large, some small, some nearer, and some farther away. Choose a moon, read its name, and locate it in the picture so you can keep following it.

---

02 · Who completes a lap?
[Compare Mimas and Titan; pause four seconds.]

Let the moons orbit and compare Mimas, farther in, with more distant Titan. Pause and predict: over the same playback interval, which makes more laps? Select each one and read its orbital period. The moons share one Saturn, but they do not travel as a matching parade at one speed. Each has its own rhythm.

---

03 · Two unusual worlds
[Select Titan and Enceladus in turn and read their discoveries; do not switch to a close-up or show simulated plumes.]

Select Titan and read the spacecraft discoveries. It has a thick atmosphere and lakes of liquid, but that liquid is not the water we drink. Next select Enceladus: it has a bright icy shell and sprays water vapor and icy grains from its southern region. Spacecraft discovered these features with careful pictures and measurements. The spheres help us locate the moons; they do not unfold those landscapes or plumes in the picture.

---

04 · Read their physical sizes
[Select Titan and Enceladus in turn to compare diameter readouts. Keep the orbit view and explain that spheres and spacing do not share a true length scale.]

Finally, compare the diameter readouts. Select Titan and note its diameter, then read Enceladus’s diameter. Titan is much larger. To keep the moons visible, the picture adjusts body sizes and distances separately; its spheres and gaps cannot be used as a ruler. These worlds hold many questions to investigate. Even where there is water, we cannot immediately conclude that something is living there.
