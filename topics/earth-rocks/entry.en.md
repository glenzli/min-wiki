## how many different journeys can a rock take? {#rocks}

### Are rocks big pieces and minerals small pieces?

No. A mineral has a characteristic composition or compositional range and an ordered internal structure: quartz, feldspar, mica and calcite are examples. A rock is an aggregate of one or more minerals or other geological material. A small broken piece of granite may still contain several minerals; becoming smaller does not turn it into a single mineral. [USGS: rocks and minerals](https://www.usgs.gov/faqs/what-difference-between-a-rock-and-a-mineral)

In the granite example, look for glassy quartz, lighter feldspar and platy mica. Calcite is a major mineral in many limestones and marbles. Real specimens vary in color and proportions. Color alone is not a mineral's identity card: crystal form, hardness, cleavage, mineral associations and geological context all provide evidence. A photograph alone cannot guarantee identification.

### Branch hall: what about crystals, ores and gemstones?

A mineral can have a repeating internal structure while its outward shape depends on growth space, conditions and later damage. Missing attractive crystal faces does not mean missing crystal structure. An ore is natural material from which valuable constituents can be extracted under particular technical and economic conditions; it may contain several minerals. Gemstone describes material valued for decorative use and its properties, rather than a category mutually exclusive with minerals or rocks. Bauxite is an important example: it is a major aluminium ore and a rock containing several minerals, rather than a single mineral. [BGS: bauxite and material uses](https://www.bgs.ac.uk/discovering-geology/maps-and-resources/critical-raw-materials-resources/modern-life-doesnt-grow-on-trees/critical-minerals-in-infrastructure/)

Visit [Minerals and Crystals](../earth-minerals/entry.en.md) to compare lattice illustrations, properties, formation environments and uses, and combine evidence as specimen researchers do. Color or shine in an image cannot certify an identification. The colored balls and rods in a lattice model are also not literal colored atoms and sticks that a stronger magnifier would reveal.

### Do the three main rock groups describe color or origin?

They describe principal formation processes. These specimens begin the comparison; they are not all the rocks in the world.

| Specimen | Group and formation clues | What to inspect and qualify |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Granite | Igneous; melt crystallized underground | Often coarse-grained and multi-mineral; composition and cooling conditions both matter |
| Basalt | Igneous; formed through cooling basaltic lava and related processes | Often fine-grained; chemically different from granite, rather than merely faster-cooled granite |
| Sandstone | Clastic sedimentary; sand deposited and lithified | Grains, pores and cement; not simply another name for loose sand |
| Limestone | Sedimentary; often accumulated biological carbonate or chemical precipitation | Large shells need not be visible; not all sedimentary rock forms by pressing sand |
| Slate | Metamorphic; fine-grained parent rock transformed | Cleavage may be present; it is different from sedimentary bedding |
| Marble | Metamorphic; limestone and related material recrystallized | Foliation and stripes are not required in every metamorphic rock |
| Gneiss | Metamorphic; distinct mineral bands in some examples | Light and dark bands are clues, but interpreting history needs more evidence |

Igneous classification considers composition and crystallization conditions together. Sedimentary rocks include clastic, biological and chemical routes. Metamorphic rocks include foliated and non-foliated examples. [NPS igneous rocks](https://www.nps.gov/subjects/geology/igneous.htm), [sedimentary rocks](https://www.nps.gov/subjects/geology/sedimentary.htm), [metamorphic rocks](https://www.nps.gov/subjects/geology/metamorphic.htm)

One useful naming distinction: **schist is a metamorphic rock; tuff forms from volcanic ash and related fragments.** They are not two translations of the same rock. Similar names or colors cannot replace formation evidence.

### Does metamorphism mean melting and freezing again?

Metamorphism mainly involves solid-state recrystallization, mineral reactions and structural change caused by temperature, pressure and fluids. Some platy or elongated minerals align to make foliation; marble need not be foliated. If material melts, the melt later makes igneous products as it cools. Real settings can contain solid residues alongside partial melt, making their history more complicated than three classification cards. [USGS: metamorphism without melting](https://www.usgs.gov/faqs/what-are-metamorphic-rocks)

### Why is the journey not a required circle?

Material can remain, return or branch as conditions change. Weathering breaks down or chemically changes material in place. Erosion and transport remove it from its original location. Deposition accumulates sediment; lithification makes loose deposits into rock. These related processes are different actions. [BGS: connected rock-cycle processes](https://www.bgs.ac.uk/discovering-geology/rocks-and-minerals/)

Try different routes:

- **Stay at the surface:** without a changed condition, material need not automatically turn into another rock group.
- **Make clastic sedimentary rock:** weather into particles, transport them to a basin, then bury, compact and cement them.
- **Change while solid underground:** burial followed by suitable heat and pressure can make metamorphic products; uplift and erosion can later expose them again.
- **Melt, then crystallize:** after granitic material melts, this example compares slower underground cooling with faster surface cooling to form coarse granite or finer rhyolite. These have similar compositions. Basalt is not the result of simply cooling that same granitic melt faster.

The markers track material provenance, not indestructible mineral balls. Dissolution, reactions and melting can destroy original crystals. The same material's ancestry is different from one crystal surviving intact. The journey does not predict how many years each process takes.

**Explore together:** Stop at loose sediment and explain what it still needs to become sandstone. Return to buried solid material and choose metamorphism or melting. Which keeps the material solid? Look for grains, bedding and foliation in existing specimens or photographs, and explain what their appearance alone cannot establish.

## Formation processes distinguish the main rock groups

Igneous rock crystallizes from cooling melt. Burial, compaction and cementation can form clastic sedimentary rock, while biological and chemical routes form other sedimentary rocks. Metamorphism mainly involves solid-state recrystallization, reactions and structural change, rather than melting followed by cooling. Foliation, bedding and grains provide different kinds of clues.

## Cooling rate cannot replace composition

Crystallization conditions affect grain size for a given melt composition. Here granitic material compares coarse granite from slower underground cooling with finer rhyolite from faster surface cooling. Basalt has a different composition and is not simply faster-cooled granitic melt. Real rocks can also record several crystallization stages and other conditions.

## Minerals, rocks and ores answer different questions

A mineral has a compositional range and ordered internal structure. A rock aggregates minerals or other geological material. Ore concerns extraction of useful constituents under particular technical and economic conditions and may contain several minerals. Bauxite is a rock and an important aluminium ore, not one mineral. Photographs and color offer clues without certifying identification.

## A common misconception

Rocks have no compulsory igneous-sedimentary-metamorphic circuit. Metamorphism is not melting, and not all metamorphic rocks are foliated. Schist and tuff are different rocks. Basalt and granite differ in more than cooling rate. Material markers are not intact mineral balls that survive melting.

## Model limits

This branching teaching journey uses prerequisite conditions without predicting natural rates, exact pressure-temperature thresholds or mineral equilibria. Fixed markers track provenance, not unaltered crystals, atoms or complete chemistry through every natural process. Grains and bands are representative illustrations. Specimen views explain typical properties without guaranteeing real-object identification. The Minerals hall develops crystal structure and ore conditions further.

### What else is inside a rock?

Granite can contain several different minerals. Quartz, feldspar and mica are not named differently just because their pieces differ in size. Mineral structure and composition connect with another question: how did the whole rock form?

*Scene cue: Compare granite with enlarged quartz, feldspar and mica illustrations; explain that color alone cannot determine identity.*

### Water and wind move particles

Rock can change slowly in place, or break into particles carried away by water, wind or gravity. After settling elsewhere, burial, compaction and cementation may turn the particles into new sedimentary rock.

*Scene cue: Stop at loose hillslope particles before showing transport to a basin and lithification. Distinguish weathering, transport and deposition.*

### Change while solid, or melt first

Buried rock sometimes remains solid while its minerals and structure change. Other conditions can cause melting. When that melt cools, it crystallizes into rock again. These routes involve different processes.

*Scene cue: Branch from the same buried state into metamorphism or melting, then compare cooling routes for one melt composition.*

### There is no required destination

Material with the same ancestry can stay for a long time or be uplifted, weathered and buried again. We trace its provenance without promising that every original crystal survives. Before recording the next step, ask whether its conditions really changed.

*Scene cue: Retain the same material markers, compare staying and branching, and connect to structure, properties and evidence in the Minerals hall.*
