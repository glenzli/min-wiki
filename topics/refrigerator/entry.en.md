## Moving heat requires work

Heat can flow spontaneously from warmer to cooler matter. Moving it continuously from a cold cabinet into a warmer room requires work. Over a complete cycle, heat rejected equals heat absorbed plus input work: Qh = Qc + W. A cooling coefficient Qc/W above one measures heat moved per unit of work, not energy creation. With the door open and both sides acting on the same room, the electrical input ultimately adds heat to that room.

## Pressure and phase change in a sealed circuit

Refrigerant absorbs heat and vaporizes in the low-pressure evaporator. The compressor raises vapor pressure and temperature. The condenser releases heat into the room and condenses the refrigerant. A capillary tube or another throttling device reduces pressure; some liquid flashes into vapor before returning to the evaporator. Heat crosses the pipe wall, but refrigerant has no route into the food. Fluids, component locations and metering devices differ among designs; colors only identify parts.

## Heat load and temperature control

Wall conduction, door air exchange and warm food increase the load. Air and lining respond before the food interior, whose cooling also depends on mass, shape, conduction and convection. Two lumped heat capacities and separate start/stop thresholds illustrate delay here. A fixed-speed compressor stops after sufficient cooling and restarts as temperature rises; variable-speed designs can adjust capacity. The curves show trends, not when a particular food reaches safe storage conditions.

## A common misconception

A refrigerator does not make a substance called cold that enters food. Refrigerant stays in pipes while heat crosses their walls.

## Model limits

One refrigerated space is represented using a fixed room temperature, simplified heat capacities, constant running power and on/off control. Moisture, defrosting, complex airflow and refrigerant equations of state are omitted. The expanded pipes, compressed times, temperatures and powers do not represent a measured appliance or provide food-storage or repair guidance.

### Where did the heat go?

We put a bowl of food in the refrigerator. After a while, it slowly feels cooler. Where did the heat that kept it warm go? The refrigerator is like a helper that carries heat from inside to the room outside. Look at the whole refrigerator first, then gently choose See inside. We have not taken off a real door. The picture is helping us look through it.

*Scene cue: Hold the kitchen view for two seconds, then choose See inside.*

### Food cools slowly

There is food here, and air all around it. Start watching. The air cools first, but the middle of the food takes longer. Pause and find the two lines. Which changes faster? Now choose the scene with the opening door. Warmer room air comes in, giving the refrigerator more heat to carry away. After the door closes, cooling still takes time.

*Scene cue: Play and pause the closed-door scene, then compare both lines during the opening-door scene.*

### Around the pipes

Follow the gold circle into the winding pipes. Inside is a fluid that helps carry heat, called refrigerant. First it takes in heat inside the cabinet and becomes gas. The compressor uses electricity to help, making it hotter. Outside the cabinet, it gives heat to the room and becomes liquid again. A narrow tube then lowers its pressure, and it can come back to help again.

*Scene cue: Choose Follow the pipes, play one circuit slowly and pause at each numbered part.*

### What if the door stays open?

The refrigerant stays inside its sealed pipes. It does not run into your dinner. The room gets a little more heat than the refrigerator took from the food, because the electricity the machine uses counts too. Leaving the door open cannot make the whole room cooler. Our observation is finished. Can you point to where the heat started and where it ended up?

*Scene cue: Pause at the heat-releasing pipe, trace from cabinet to room and allow three seconds to answer.*
