Would keeping a duck’s foot fully spread make its return stroke harder?

Play one complete stroke, pausing or scrubbing anywhere. Compare toe spread, the backward push, folding and recovery. The enlarged foot and the foot below the duck show the same stroke.

## Webbing changes the area interacting with water

Webbing joins the toes and creates a larger effective area when spread. Force depends on speed and orientation as well as area. With comparable motion, spreading the webbing moves more water; folding changes the recovery shape. Muscles supply the energy, while structure affects how that effort interacts with water.

## Pushing water produces a reaction on the duck

The foot gives water backward momentum, and the water exerts a reaction on the foot that helps propel the body. Flow, body drag and both feet affect the resulting motion. Paired forces do not imply equal speeds for duck and water, since their masses, surroundings and other forces differ.

## Recovery reduces the cost of returning the foot

A broad foot pulled forward through water can oppose forward motion. Changing spread and orientation reduces this recovery cost. The three stages separate a continuous stroke for inspection. Real ducks vary rhythm, use both feet and turn. The no-webbing comparison isolates shape, not a calibrated prediction of swimming speed.

## A common misconception

Webbing is not an engine, and larger feet do not automatically mean faster swimming. The important comparison is spreading during propulsion and reducing area during recovery.

## Model limits

The 16-second sequence slows one stroke for inspection; it is not a measured stroke period. Water marks, arrows and displacement are not a fluid simulation. Hiding webbing compares shape, not swimming speed.

### Look between the toes

A duck glides across a pond while its feet work below the surface. Between its toes is skin called webbing. When the toes spread, the webbing opens like a broad little paddle. But the duck does not keep that paddle fully open all the time. Let us inspect one stroke. Press play to connect opening, pushing and recovery. Pause whenever you like, or drag the progress control to look back.

*Scene cue: Play one complete stroke, pausing during propulsion and recovery. The foot returns to readiness while the body retains forward displacement.*

### Push against the water

Let the foot sweep backward. The broad foot pushes water back, and the water pushes on the foot to help move the duck forward. Pause and inspect the two arrows. They show related directions of action, not a promise that duck and water travel at the same speed.

*Scene cue: Pause during propulsion and compare the arrows.*

### Return the foot with less resistance

After pushing, the foot must come forward for another stroke. The toes draw closer and present less area to the water, making recovery easier. The body does not suddenly jump back to its starting point. Connecting propulsion and recovery produces the swimming motion we see.

*Scene cue: Show foot recovery while retaining forward body displacement.*

### Compare the shapes

Hide the webbing in the drawing. The separate toes present a smaller area, do they not? This is an imagined comparison; a real duck still has webbing. We are comparing how shape helps movement, not holding a race. At a pond, watch from the bank for opening feet and turns.

*Scene cue: Hide the webbing, compare area, then restore it.*
