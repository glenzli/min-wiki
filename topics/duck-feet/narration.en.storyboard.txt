01 · Look between the toes
[Play one complete stroke, pausing during propulsion and recovery. The foot returns to readiness while the body retains forward displacement.]

A duck glides across a pond while its feet work below the surface. Between its toes is skin called webbing. When the toes spread, the webbing opens like a broad little paddle. But the duck does not keep that paddle fully open all the time. Let us inspect one stroke. Press play to connect opening, pushing and recovery. Pause whenever you like, or drag the progress control to look back.

---

02 · Push against the water
[Pause during propulsion and compare the arrows.]

Let the foot sweep backward. The broad foot pushes water back, and the water pushes on the foot to help move the duck forward. Pause and inspect the two arrows. They show related directions of action, not a promise that duck and water travel at the same speed.

---

03 · Return the foot with less resistance
[Show foot recovery while retaining forward body displacement.]

After pushing, the foot must come forward for another stroke. The toes draw closer and present less area to the water, making recovery easier. The body does not suddenly jump back to its starting point. Connecting propulsion and recovery produces the swimming motion we see.

---

04 · Compare the shapes
[Hide the webbing, compare area, then restore it.]

Hide the webbing in the drawing. The separate toes present a smaller area, do they not? This is an imagined comparison; a real duck still has webbing. We are comparing how shape helps movement, not holding a race. At a pond, watch from the bank for opening feet and turns.
