How does air surround Earth? What changes as we move outward from the same surface site?

Find the yellow surface marker. Drag or play the journey to approach the ground continuously, then move outward. Pause at layers to inspect weather, ozone, meteors and auroras, then compare another world alongside Earth.

## Layers describe temperature structure, not shells

Earth's atmosphere gradually thins with height. Temperature trends distinguish the troposphere, stratosphere, mesosphere and thermosphere, with an outer exosphere. This display uses a lower standard profile as a reference. Boundaries vary; the lower model is not extrapolated into solar-activity-dependent upper conditions.

## One surface anchor and height coordinate

The camera approaches a coastal region from a whole-Earth view, then observes outward along the same radius. The distant globe uses an Earth reference texture; near the surface, the same yellow square remains while local coastal detail becomes an illustration rather than a survey of that site. The bright dot marks observation height. A 6371 km reference Earth radius and reference layer heights share coordinates; clouds, arrows and particles are not life-size. Logarithmic height progress makes lower and upper layers accessible.

## Compare several properties of each atmosphere

Composition, total atmospheric amount, surface pressure, temperature and liquid species are distinct. Venus and Mars both have carbon-dioxide-dominated air but very different surface conditions. Titan is a moon whose surface liquid cycle mainly involves methane and ethane. Representative values and illustrations are not local live weather. The Moon and Mercury have extremely tenuous exospheres rather than Earth-like lower weather atmospheres; the display does not assign zero pressure or an invented single air temperature.

## A common misconception

Layers are not solid shells around Earth. The stratosphere does not allow only sideways motion, and high temperature does not mean auroras everywhere. The Moon and Mercury are nearly airless, not entirely particle-free.

## Model limits

An external teaching camera, not spacecraft dynamics. Globe and reference heights share distance coordinates; the distant globe uses an Earth reference map, while nearby coastal detail is illustrative rather than surveyed terrain. Clouds, arrows and particles are enlarged. Phenomena require different conditions, not a simultaneous event. Lower readings use a standard atmosphere with no upper extrapolation; planetary rows do not imply matched altitudes.

## Narration {#narration}

### Look at the whole Earth

We live on a round Earth. From far away, air hugs its edge. Remember the yellow marker: we will keep watching the same surface site.

*Scene cue: Approach slowly from the globe while following the same yellow square.*

### Approach winds and clouds

Now the ground and low clouds become clear. Wind carries air around. Warm, moist air can rise, cool and form clouds. Droplets or ice must grow before rain or snow can fall.

*Scene cue: Pause at the surface and troposphere; identify airflow arrows and clouds and rain.*

### Keep looking outward

Higher layers do different things. Ozone absorbs some ultraviolet light. Farther up, meteors can leave luminous streaks and polar regions can show auroras. Eventually the air becomes extremely sparse and blends into space.

*Scene cue: Follow the same radial path through the stratosphere, mesosphere, thermosphere and exosphere; locate the surface square below.*

### Put another sky beside ours

Keep Earth on the left and choose another world on the right. Its clouds may be different, or it may be almost airless. The Moon and Mercury have no Earth-like weather, but a few gas particles remain.

*Scene cue: Compare Venus, Mars, Titan and the near-vacuum cases. Explain that aligned rows do not mean equal heights.*
