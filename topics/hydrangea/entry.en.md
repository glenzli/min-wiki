Will making the soil acidic turn every white hydrangea blue?

Change one condition at a time and grow a new bloom. Inspect the same progress through plant, root-transport and sepal-cell views. Compare white flowers and use the legend to identify anthocyanin, aluminium and copigment.

## Soil pH influences available aluminium

In colour-responsive bigleaf hydrangeas, acidic soil often makes aluminium more available. Aluminium must be absorbed and transported to sepal cells. Soil pH is a condition affecting availability, not a dye poured onto flowers. Suitable acidity alone does not guarantee blue when aluminium is unavailable; compare one variable at a time.

## Blue results from a chemical combination

The showy structures are mainly sepals. Blue involves complexes of anthocyanin, aluminium and copigments in vacuoles, with the cellular chemical environment also contributing. Soil pH is not vacuolar pH. Tissue mapping and transporter studies connect supply, cellular transport and visible colour.

## Cultivar and developing blooms matter

Genetic background determines whether this colour response is possible. Acidifying soil does not supply missing anthocyanin to a white cultivar, and other hydrangea species may behave differently. Uptake, transport and maturation take time, so settings for the next bloom are kept distinct from the current bloom.

## A common misconception

Soil acidity is not a universal flower-colour switch; available aluminium, pigments, cultivar and development all matter.

## Model limits

This represents part of the bigleaf-hydrangea colour mechanism. Bars and aluminium percentages are qualitative, not gardening recipes or dosing advice. Gold-dot counts, routes and the 14-second cycle illustrate causality, not measured concentrations, detailed transport anatomy or reaction rates. Green squares are copigments, not chloroplasts.

### A garden question

Hydrangeas can look like soft clouds of blue, pink or white. You may hear that changing the soil changes their colour. That can happen, but several conditions matter. Today we will grow a new bloom in our picture and compare what happens under different settings.

*Scene cue: Show the mature flower head and the condition controls.*

### Change one thing at a time

Keep the cultivar the same, adjust the soil pH and grow a bloom. Pause and remember its colour. Next change only the aluminium supply and grow another. Aluminium here is something the plant can obtain from soil, not blue paint. Soil conditions influence how available it is.

*Scene cue: Compare pH and aluminium separately, pausing between blooms.*

### Inside the colour space

First follow the roots and transport. Gold dots represent available aluminium, traveling through roots and stem toward a sepal. Inside a cell, pink ovals represent anthocyanin and green squares are helper pigments. Together with aluminium they can form a blue complex. These little shapes are identification symbols. Uptake, transport and new growth take time; garden flowers do not instantly change their clothes.

*Scene cue: Review uptake in the root view, switch to the cell at the same progress, identify all three legend symbols and watch them assemble together.*

### Invite a white-flowered control

Try the white cultivar. It does not follow the same route to blue because the needed coloured pigment is missing. Colour depends on the plant as well as its surroundings. The useful habit in our experiment is changing one thing, waiting and comparing carefully before deciding what the result means.

*Scene cue: Grow a white cultivar and compare with the pigmented cultivar.*
