## Tides measure a difference

Tides compare gravity at different positions within an object, rather than just its central acceleration. For stellar radius R much smaller than distance r, the radial acceleration difference from the center to the near side is approximately 2GMR/r³. Comparing this with the star’s self-gravity gives the order-of-magnitude tidal radius rₜ≈R(M/m)^(1/3). Orbit and internal structure affect the actual outcome.

## Fallback, circularization and accretion are distinct

Different parts acquire different orbital energies and angular momenta. Some can escape, while bound debris can return on elongated, eccentric orbits. Stream interactions and shocks may dissipate orbital energy, and angular-momentum redistribution affects inward motion. The flow may remain eccentric instead of quickly forming a complete disk. Here, gentle dissipation starts after bound debris turns inward following outward motion. A return is not a solved collision, and no fixed time forces all material inward.

## Brightness, paths and observational evidence

Orange patches follow orbital-energy loss from simplified dissipation and gradually fade. They are not temperature measurements, and fading does not necessarily mean capture. Faint lines trace a few parcels’ recent paths, not solid rings. A separate non-spinning thin-disk approximation produces the local optical image; a bright arc alone does not establish a complete disk. Real tidal disruptions are assessed using changing brightness, spectra and the host environment. Emission comes from outside the horizon, which is not identical to the optical shadow.

## A common misconception

A black hole does not vacuum up everything nearby. Distance, orbit, and internal structure affect disruption; the glowing gas is outside the horizon.

## Model limits

One continuous material population illustrates stretching, return and eccentric motion. Weakening support and dissipation after return are teaching approximations, without physical collisions, gas pressure, magnetic fields or relativistic fluid dynamics. Heating brightness, optical shadow, sizes and playback time cannot yield a real event’s temperature, captured mass or disk-formation time.

### Meet the travelers

Let’s travel with a star. A star is a huge ball of hot gas held together by its own gravity. The black hole nearby has a special boundary: once something crosses it inward, even light cannot escape. But passing nearby does not always mean falling in. First watch the farther route and see whether the star continues on its way.

*Scene cue: Show the distant pass and wait for approach.*

### Different pulls

Now bring the route closer. The side facing the black hole feels a different pull from the far side. A large enough difference can pull the star apart. Pause for a moment. Does the whole star vanish together, or do some outer layers leave first? Take your time and follow the flowing material.

*Scene cue: Pause for about four seconds when outer layers first detach.*

### Returning is another part of the journey

Keep watching as the material becomes a long, curved stream. Some travels away. Some takes a long route and comes back. Returning does not mean falling straight in or already becoming a disk. Follow the streams from above: which part is moving away, and which is returning? Faint lines mark the recent paths of a few gas parcels. They are not real rings growing around the black hole.

*Scene cue: Use the top view; follow the tail, returning streams and selected recent paths.*

### Why the bright patches change

Use the black hole close-up and find the orange patches. Real streams may collide and heat up when they meet. The changing glow helps us imagine this process, but its colors cannot tell us an exact temperature. A fading patch does not always mean the gas has fallen in. Check whether the gas is still there, then return to the overview: the long tail and curved streams are continuing their journey outside.

*Scene cue: Inspect local patches and fading in the close-up, then use the top view to locate the remaining gas.*
