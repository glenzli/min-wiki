## One continuous tract with different tasks

Food passes through the mouth, pharynx and esophagus to the stomach, then the small and large intestines. Unabsorbed contents ultimately leave through the anus. Coordinated contraction and relaxation of wall muscles produce peristaltic propulsion, while the stomach and intestine also mix contents. Stages are separated for observation; real organs can process different cohorts at the same time, and the stomach empties gradually. Screen duration cannot be converted into digestion time.

## Digestion is different from absorption

Mechanical breakdown by teeth increases exposed surface area. Saliva, gastric juice, pancreatic juice and intestinal enzymes contribute chemical digestion of digestible macromolecules into absorbable products. The liver makes bile and the gallbladder stores and releases it. Bile salts aid fat emulsification and lipid-product transport; they are not fat-cleaving enzymes. Food does not traverse liver, gallbladder or pancreas: ducts carry their contributions into the tract. The small intestine absorbs most nutrients and water; the colon continues absorption of remaining water and electrolytes.

## A villus surface and two transport routes

Villi and epithelial microvilli enlarge absorptive area while cells retain a selective barrier. Glucose and other products cross membranes into capillaries and can reach the liver through portal blood. Most dietary long-chain lipid products are rebuilt and packaged as chylomicrons inside enterocytes, entering lacteals and lymph before reaching blood; shorter-chain fats can follow different routes. The colon continues water absorption, and stool also contains microbes and shed cells.

## A common misconception

The stomach does not absorb all nutrients at once, and the liver and pancreas are not the next places food passes through. Food follows the digestive tract; digestion products cross intestinal cells before entering transport systems.

## Model limits

The organ route, local intestinal wall and villus use different scales. Particles stand for one cohort and its products, not true molecular size, composition, absorption efficiency or speed. Swallowing detail, microvilli, transport proteins and complex intestinal mixing are omitted. This is not a diagnostic or dietary-prescription tool.

### A bite begins its journey

Where does a bite of food go after you eat it? Start at the mouth. Teeth break it into smaller pieces, and saliva mixes with it to begin the work. After swallowing, the bite reaches the esophagus. Its wall moves in a coordinated wave to push food along. Food does not just tumble into your tummy, and it is not traveling through the tube you use to breathe.

*Scene cue: Move from mouth to esophagus at 0–26%, pointing to the gold-brown cohort on its continuous route.*

### Mixing in the stomach, with helpers nearby

Here comes the stomach. It mixes food with its digestive juice, then sends the mixture a little at a time into the small intestine. Has the stomach already taken every nutrient? Not yet. Nearby, the liver, gallbladder and pancreas help by sending digestive fluids into the small intestine. Watch carefully: food follows its own route. It does not go inside those helpers.

*Scene cue: Choose stomach mixing and then intestinal digestion; distinguish the food route from the helpers’ narrow ducts.*

### Closer to a little villus

Let us pause and move closer to the intestinal wall. Can you find the little finger-like projections called villi? Take a little time to look. They make more surface available for contact with the contents. Some nutrients have been broken into tiny parts. Those parts must cross cells in the wall before entering the body’s transport routes. A whole food chunk does not fall through a big hole.

*Scene cue: Pause at absorption, choose the villus view and allow an observation break; point to epithelial cells.*

### Nutrients enter, and the remainder moves on

Try comparing the two buttons. Some nutrients cross cells and enter blood. Some digested fats are repackaged inside cells and first take a different route called lymph, before reaching blood later. Contents that were not absorbed keep traveling into the large intestine, where more water is taken in. The remainder forms stool, passes through the rectum and leaves through the anus. Each place along this journey has its own work to do.
