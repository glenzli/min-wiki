## Three names distinguish objects and phenomena

A meteoroid is a small solid body in space, often debris from an asteroid or comet. A meteor is the luminous atmospheric phenomenon, while a meteorite is solid material that reaches the ground. None is a distant star falling. Meteor showers commonly occur when Earth meets a debris stream, and a visible streak does not guarantee recoverable meteorites.

## Light, ablation, and fragmentation

Fast entry produces strong interaction with air. Excited gas and removed material emit light, while melting, vaporization and sometimes fragmentation remove mass. The moving luminous head differs from the wake left in the air behind it, which can disperse and respond to winds. Some meteors leave longer-lasting self-emitting trains, with atmospheric chemistry helping sustain emission. A train is not a fiery tail permanently attached to a rock. Brightness depends on speed, mass, composition and atmosphere, not size alone.

## Dark flight may continue after the glow

Some strong fragments survive the luminous high-speed stage. After deceleration ends intense emission, they can continue falling under gravity and aerodynamic drag: dark flight. Winds also influence their landing locations. Survival depends on speed, angle, strength, shape, and mass, not simply large versus small. The two page examples illustrate possible endings, not a universal size rule.

## A common misconception

A meteor is not a star falling. Losing its glow does not necessarily mean all material has vanished. A meteorite is surviving solid material that reaches the ground.

## Model limits

Path, slowing, material loss and wake spreading use prescribed teaching curves, without solving aerodynamics, ablation or chemistry. Sizes, altitudes and stage durations are not to scale. The visible-wake examples do not imply every meteor has a persistent train, and the two endings do not predict survival or landing location from size.

### What makes that bright line?

Sometimes a bright line flashes across the night sky. We call it a meteor, or a shooting star, but a distant star has not fallen. The traveler is often a small piece of space debris. While it is still in space, it is called a meteoroid. Let’s follow the little fragment in our picture.

*Scene cue: Advance slowly from the space starting point.*

### Meeting the air

The fragment rushes into Earth’s atmosphere and meets the air very strongly. It can glow while losing some of its material. Pause and find where the light begins. Now move a little farther. The bright head has moved on, but the wake stays in the air it just passed through and slowly spreads. It is not a string of little stars following the rock.

*Scene cue: Choose “See the glowing stage”, pause for four seconds, then slowly advance progress to compare the head with its wake.*

### Not every trip reaches the ground

Some fragments disappear along the way and leave no rock on the ground. Others leave a piece that slows down, stops glowing brightly, and keeps falling. Try both examples. When the light disappears, has the fragment really gone, or has it simply stopped shining? Look carefully for the remaining piece.

*Scene cue: Choose “After the glow” and switch journeys at the same progress to compare disappearance with dark flight.*

### Three names for the journey

If a solid piece reaches the ground, it is called a meteorite. Put the names together: a meteoroid in space, a meteor’s bright atmospheric streak, and perhaps a meteorite left at the end. Not every journey reaches that final stage. Now a flashing line in the sky can make you wonder what is happening, without thinking a star has fallen.

*Scene cue: Finish at the endpoint and compare the three terms.*
