## The structure of the Milky Way

The Milky Way is a barred spiral with a flattened stellar disk, central bulge and bar, sparse stellar halo, gas and other components. The Sun lies in the disk about 26,000 light-years from the centre; a rounded 50,000-light-year disk radius provides a structural reference here. Changing the view changes projection rather than reshuffling stars: face-on reveals arms, while edge-on compares disk and halo depth. Spiral, elliptical and irregular are morphological cases, not a compulsory evolutionary sequence. Star clusters are stellar collections that can belong to a galaxy; galaxy groups contain multiple galaxies. Dot counts and roundness do not define a universal classification. Dark matter is inferred from gravity; the visible halo dots in this chapter represent the stellar halo only. The central black hole governs nearby orbits, not the whole galaxy as a giant Solar System. The sky band is an inside-disk view. Constellations are sky regions and projected patterns, not galaxies. Orion uses J2000 directions and selected reciprocal 2007 Hipparcos parallaxes. Bright and multiple-star distances differ between studies; bars show only formal parallax errors. Some stars can share associations; pattern lines alone establish no physical link. M31 and M87 are real examples, while their point volumes remain structural illustrations. The Sun is a star; sky lights may also be reflecting planets or satellites, or unresolved multiple systems. M31 has distinctive dust and star-forming rings. The irregular example uses observations of Barnard’s Galaxy, NGC 6822. A common length conversion uses selected stellar-body spans of about 100,000, 200,000 and 120,000 light-years for the Milky Way, M31 and a bright M87 body; NGC 6822 uses about 7,600 light-years from 0.50 Mpc and a 16-arcminute major axis. These are not uniform-isophote boundaries or rankings of complete halos or total masses.

## A reconstruction with defined limits

The external Milky Way view is reconstructed from observations made inside it, not photographed from outside. Beyond the Milky Way, the Local Group includes Andromeda, Triangulum and many dwarf galaxies such as the Magellanic Clouds. The Virgo Cluster is a separate nearby structure, not a container for the Local Group. In a Laniakea flow reconstruction both occupy the wider region, but its boundary depends on observations and the model, and the supercluster is generally not bound as a whole. The cosmic web is a pattern of large-scale distribution, not a separate shell around Laniakea. Address diagrams are not to scale; the observable-region view and the continuous camera's endpoint share one statistical illustration, and the central locator is not the supercluster's diameter. Distant filaments, knots and points are not measured positions or sizes of individual galaxies and cannot be measured from the picture. The far-end length is an approximate present-day spatial distance, not light-travel time; expansion and lookback time are not simulated.

## A common misconception

A light-year is a distance. Solar systems, star clusters, galaxies and galaxy groups are different levels. An edge-on view does not change a galaxy’s category, and morphology is not a compulsory evolutionary sequence. An observer-centred observable region does not make Earth the physical centre of the universe; its horizon is not a wall. The Andromeda sky region is not the Andromeda galaxy. Nearby-looking stars need not be equally distant. TRAPPIST-1 and the Solar System are planetary systems; M31 and the Milky Way are galaxies. A star, a stellar system and a galaxy are different concepts; one unresolved point need not contain one star. Kepler-16 has a confirmed circumbinary planet. Cygnus X-1 is a star–black-hole binary; no unconfirmed planet is added.

### From sky to galaxies

Begin with the sky. The Sun is a star, and it is our nearest one. Many night-sky points are distant Milky Way stars; some lights are reflecting planets, or multiple stars we cannot separate. The Milky Way band blends light from many stars. We first look from inside its disk, then outside, to see where the band comes from. Our galaxy has differently shaped neighbours. Comparing their selected sizes needs one length ruler.

*Scene cue: Change from ground sky to the along-disk view; explore named-star distances, the whole Milky Way and common-scale galaxy comparisons.*

### Inward to our home

Locate the Sun in the galaxy and approach nearby stars, the Solar System, Earth and Moon, and finally Earth. A separate comparison window explores different stellar families. Some planets orbit one star, while others travel around two. A star may also have a black-hole companion. Planets reflect starlight; bright gas near a black hole is not light emitted by the black hole itself.

*Scene cue: Approach Earth through the inward stops. Separately play Kepler-16 or Cygnus X-1, then return to Earth and Moon.*

### Outward to the cosmos

Look outward from the Milky Way. Our galaxy and many others belong to the Local Group. The nearby Virgo Cluster is another structure to compare. Associations across still wider regions rely on observations and models. The cosmic web is a large-scale distribution, not a glowing shell. As the scale grows, one locator may stand for an entire galaxy; a drawn point does not always mean one star.

*Scene cue: Explore the galaxy, group, neighbouring cluster, supercluster, cosmic web and observable region, distinguishing members, neighbours and statistical illustrations.*

### Earth to the universe

Finally zoom continuously from Earth. At first we can see its outline; later we need a locator. The Solar System, Milky Way and larger structures enter the view as the length ruler changes. The final dashed reference describes our observing range, not a wall around the whole universe. It does not make Earth the centre of the universe.

*Scene cue: Play the fourth chapter, pause or reverse it, and compare the physical ruler, location anchor and current model limits.*
