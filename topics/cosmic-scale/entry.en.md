## One length system across many orders of magnitude

Distances remain in kilometers. The slider changes the logarithm of view width; it does not separately take logarithms of body radii. Geometry within each frame remains a linear projection. Kilometers, AU and light-years are all length units. Subpixel bodies switch to explicit position symbols rather than silently enlarged disks. VY Canis Majoris uses a 2012 photospheric estimate of about 1420 solar radii. St2-18 uses about 2150 solar radii inferred from 2012 luminosity and temperature values, conditional on uncertain distance and cluster membership. These are estimated examples, not a largest-star ranking.

## The structure of the Milky Way

Our galaxy includes a stellar disk, central bulge and bar, stellar halo, gas and other components. The Sun lies in its disk around 26,000 light-years from the center. A dark-matter halo is inferred indirectly from gravitational effects rather than seen in starlight. The central black hole dominates nearby orbits, not the entire galaxy as a single-point-mass Solar System.

## A reconstruction with defined limits

The external Milky Way view is reconstructed from observations made inside it, not photographed from outside. Beyond the Milky Way, the Local Group includes Andromeda, Triangulum and around 50 dwarf galaxies such as the Magellanic Clouds. The Virgo Cluster is a separate nearby structure, not a container for the Local Group. In a Laniakea flow reconstruction both occupy the wider region, but its boundary depends on observations and the model, and the supercluster is generally not bound as a whole. The cosmic web is a pattern of large-scale distribution, not a separate shell around Laniakea. Address diagrams are not to scale; the observable-region view and the continuous camera's endpoint share one statistical illustration, and the central locator is not the supercluster's diameter. Distant filaments, knots and points are not measured positions or sizes of individual galaxies and cannot be measured from the picture. The far-end length is an approximate present-day spatial distance, not light-travel time; expansion and lookback time are not simulated.

## A common misconception

A light-year is a distance, not a duration. The Solar System is not the Milky Way, and the disk edge is not the universe’s boundary. The central black hole is not the galaxy’s only source of gravity.

## Model limits

Earth’s surface, individual point distribution and external galaxy appearance are illustrations. Crosshairs and tiny open circles show locations, not inflated physical sizes. Directions are schematic, halo limits are not precise boundaries, and the small-galaxy marks are a sample, not a Local Group census. Body comparisons show diameters, not masses or separations; Arcturus uses an estimated 25.4 solar radii. Structure diagrams have no size or distance scale, and filaments and points in the continuous long-range camera cannot be measured with its ruler. The observable horizon is not the physical edge of the whole universe.

### Put two bodies side by side

Earth looks small beside Jupiter. Bring in the Sun and Jupiter becomes small; bring in Arcturus and even the Sun looks small. We compare diameters on one shared ruler. Placing them together does not mean they are physically close. Now meet enormous stars with catalogue-like names. With the estimates shown here, one placed at the Sun could reach around Saturn’s orbit. First imagine that size; then explore how astronomers estimate it.

*Scene cue: Choose the three pairs or drag the slider, tracking the same retained body.*

### A bigger ball or a bigger family?

The Sun and its orbiting planets belong to the Solar System. Our Sun is one of many stars in the Milky Way. The Milky Way, Andromeda and many smaller galaxies belong to the Local Group. The Local Group and Virgo Cluster are different neighbors, yet both appear in the reconstructed Laniakea flow region. The cosmic web describes how such structures are distributed; it is not an outer shell.

*Scene cue: Open the membership chapter and follow the five-stop address to the observable region; explore Virgo and a local web close-up separately.*

### Now really look outward

Remember those sizes, then choose Earth or the Sun and zoom out. Fitting distant orbits into the view makes even the Sun tiny. An open circle helps us find it without secretly making its physical disk larger.

### Beyond our galaxy

The Milky Way is not alone. The Local Group has many small galaxies, while the Virgo Cluster is a separate neighbor. The camera keeps pulling back through a cosmic-web illustration to our observing horizon. That horizon is not a wall around the universe. Distant filaments and points are illustrative, and today's distance is not the light's travel time.
