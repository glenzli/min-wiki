01 · From sky to galaxies
[Change from ground sky to the along-disk view; explore named-star distances, the whole Milky Way and common-scale galaxy comparisons.]

Begin with the sky. The Sun is a star, and it is our nearest one. Many night-sky points are distant Milky Way stars; some lights are reflecting planets, or multiple stars we cannot separate. The Milky Way band blends light from many stars. We first look from inside its disk, then outside, to see where the band comes from. Our galaxy has differently shaped neighbours. Comparing their selected sizes needs one length ruler.

---

02 · Inward to our home
[Approach Earth through the inward stops. Separately play Kepler-16 or Cygnus X-1, then return to Earth and Moon.]

Locate the Sun in the galaxy and approach nearby stars, the Solar System, Earth and Moon, and finally Earth. A separate comparison window explores different stellar families. Some planets orbit one star, while others travel around two. A star may also have a black-hole companion. Planets reflect starlight; bright gas near a black hole is not light emitted by the black hole itself.

---

03 · Outward to the cosmos
[Explore the galaxy, group, neighbouring cluster, supercluster, cosmic web and observable region, distinguishing members, neighbours and statistical illustrations.]

Look outward from the Milky Way. Our galaxy and many others belong to the Local Group. The nearby Virgo Cluster is another structure to compare. Associations across still wider regions rely on observations and models. The cosmic web is a large-scale distribution, not a glowing shell. As the scale grows, one locator may stand for an entire galaxy; a drawn point does not always mean one star.

---

04 · Earth to the universe
[Play the fourth chapter, pause or reverse it, and compare the physical ruler, location anchor and current model limits.]

Finally zoom continuously from Earth. At first we can see its outline; later we need a locator. The Solar System, Milky Way and larger structures enter the view as the length ruler changes. The final dashed reference describes our observing range, not a wall around the whole universe. It does not make Earth the centre of the universe.
