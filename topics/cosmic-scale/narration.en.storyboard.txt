01 · Put two bodies side by side
[Choose the three pairs or drag the slider, tracking the same retained body.]

Earth looks small beside Jupiter. Bring in the Sun and Jupiter becomes small; bring in Arcturus and even the Sun looks small. We compare diameters on one shared ruler. Placing them together does not mean they are physically close. Now meet enormous stars with catalogue-like names. With the estimates shown here, one placed at the Sun could reach around Saturn’s orbit. First imagine that size; then explore how astronomers estimate it.

02 · A bigger ball or a bigger family?
[Open the membership chapter and follow the five-stop address to the observable region; explore Virgo and a local web close-up separately.]

The Sun and its orbiting planets belong to the Solar System. Our Sun is one of many stars in the Milky Way. The Milky Way, Andromeda and many smaller galaxies belong to the Local Group. The Local Group and Virgo Cluster are different neighbors, yet both appear in the reconstructed Laniakea flow region. The cosmic web describes how such structures are distributed; it is not an outer shell.

03 · Turn the Milky Way around
[Open galaxy structure, choose face-on and edge-on, then drag to rotate. Toggle the disk, bulge and stellar halo; compare the cluster membership context with its independent close-up.]

Find the Sun locator in the Milky Way. Face-on, starlight traces arms; turn edge-on and the same stars reveal a thin disk, a central bulge and a sparse stellar halo. The Sun stays in the disk. Compare a smooth elliptical galaxy and an asymmetric irregular galaxy. These are different examples, not stages of one galaxy changing shape. A star cluster is a collection of stars within a galaxy, not simply a miniature galaxy.

04 · Beyond our galaxy
[Open continuous zoom, travel from the selected body through the Milky Way, Local Group and Laniakea distance scales to the observable region, and compare the linear ruler with the location labels.]

Keep the Sun’s place in the Milky Way in mind, then zoom continuously from Earth or the Sun. Fitting distant orbits into the view makes even the Sun’s disk invisible; its locator does not secretly enlarge it. The Milky Way shares the Local Group with many small galaxies, while Virgo is a separate neighbour. Farther out, the cosmic web illustrates a distribution. The final dashed horizon marks what can be observed from our location, not a wall around the universe. Today’s distance is not the light’s travel time.
