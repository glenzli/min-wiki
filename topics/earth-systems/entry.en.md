Why does seawater form layers, and how does it exchange water and heat?

## Density connects temperature and salt

At the same reference pressure within this seawater experiment's range, cooling and increasing salinity usually increase density. Their effects can reinforce or counteract each other. Real exchange between surface and deep oceans also involves wind, rotation, topography, mixing and upwelling. The global conveyor illustrates connections, rather than a sealed pipe followed by every drop. Reference density uses the surface form of historical EOS-80 with the required temperature-scale conversion, limited here to 5-25 degrees Celsius and practical salinity 30-38, rather than a complete ocean state.

## Many connections join surface and deep water

Wind drives many surface currents. Cooling and salinity changes in some high-latitude regions contribute to deep-water formation. Upwelling and mixing help return deeper water. Rotation, coasts and seafloor shape also affect pathways; circulation is not a closed pipe every water parcel must follow.

## What the density comparison establishes

This study compares density at the same surface reference pressure. Two readouts do not calculate sinking speed, actual depth, global currents or climate. Continuous water regions and circle/diamond marks track origin; the dashed boundary is an explanation cue, not a divider. The geometry is reversible and seven display seconds have no physical time calibration. Colors do not encode actual temperature; marks are not molecules or measured ocean water masses.

## A common misconception

Cold water is not necessarily denser than every warm sample: compare salinity too. Water does not automatically rise on reaching the tropics, and no parcel must complete one universal ocean circuit.

## Model limits

Density uses a surface-reference form within the study’s selected temperature and salinity range. Geometry explains relative layering and connections without solving real mixing, velocities or global circulation or predicting future climate. ### Compare equal volumes of water

Two equal-sized seawater samples can have different masses and therefore different densities. Temperature and salt both matter. If colder water is also much fresher, compare both changes together.

### The surface and deep ocean connect

Winds drive many surface currents. In some places, cooling and increasing salt contribute to deep-water formation. Mixing and upwelling help water return. The ocean has many branches, rather than one pipe wrapped around Earth.

*Scene cue: Expand the ocean connections diagram. Distinguish wind-driven surface motion, sinking in some regions and return through upwelling and mixing, without promising a unique closed trajectory.*

### Combine two changes

Now make the water both colder and fresher. Their effects can oppose one another. Read temperature and salinity in the same comparison instead of judging by color alone.
