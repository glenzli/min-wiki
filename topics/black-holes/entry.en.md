If a black hole is dark, why can we see light around it?

Select the horizon, shadow, disk and light-bending explanations, then change viewpoint. Compare encounters with ongoing supply; in the scale chapter, zoom out to Sagittarius A* in our galactic center and the larger M87*.

## Horizon versus shadow

The horizon is a causal boundary; the shadow is an optical projection involving captured and bent light. For a distant observer and a non-spinning hole, the critical projected scale is about 2.60 horizon radii. The geometric diagram distinguishes them without exposing the interior.

## Emitting matter and its image

The luminous disk lies outside the hole. Bent light produces the apparent arches above and below it. Thin-disk, non-spinning and finite-integration assumptions constrain the display; its colors, brightness and playback speed are not observational measurements.

## From encounters to physical scales

The stellar and planetary chapters explore individual tidal encounters, while the companion chapter explores ongoing supply. Each retains its own model, scale and limitations. Compare mechanisms rather than on-screen sizes or particle counts. The scale chapter compares a hypothetical 10-solar-mass hole, Sagittarius A* at about 4 million solar masses and M87* at about 6.5 billion. A consistent non-spinning-equivalent radius rₛ = 2GM/c² scales linearly with mass. Spin, observational uncertainty and optical shadows are not included. Concentric overlays compare sizes, not actual locations.

## A common misconception

The disk is not a black-hole surface, and a shadow edge is not simply the event horizon. Passing, disruption, return and infall are distinct processes.

## Model limits

This project illustrates structures and mechanisms, not a complete simulation of an observed system. Matter dynamics and optics use separate approximations and display scales. Chapters use different educational scales. On-screen sizes, particle counts, colors and playback seconds are not real measurements.

### Look at the whole scene

Find the dark center and the surrounding light. Hot gas outside the black hole makes that light.

*Scene cue: Start with an unlabelled close-up and allow time to observe.*

### Identify the boundary

Beyond the horizon, outward signals cannot reach faraway observers. Blue lines in this diagram simply help us locate the boundary.

*Scene cue: Select the horizon, then the shadow, to compare the representations.*

### Change direction

Light from the far side bends toward us. The bright band looks arched, although the disk has not stood upright.

*Scene cue: Switch from the angled close-up to the top view, then rotate freely.*

### Compare encounters and sizes

Next, let a star or planet pass nearby. Then see how a companion keeps supplying gas over time. Black holes come in very different sizes. Sagittarius A* at our galactic center is enormously massive, and M87 has a bigger one still. Zoom out slowly: the little circle we could see is now almost invisible. It has not vanished. Our ruler has changed.
