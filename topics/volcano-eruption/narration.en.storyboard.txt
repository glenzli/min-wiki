01 · Choose a volcano
[Show the five case cards, enter the Omuroyama-style cone and open the cutaway.]

Volcanoes have many shapes. A broad mountain, a round cone, a mountain under the sea and even a lake can tell a volcanic story. Choose one and open its cutaway. Follow magma to see whether it reaches the surface, then what remains.

---

02 · Erupted material stays
[Advance deposition through cooling, compare the three shapes, then pause within the eruption detail.]

Look underground first. New magma enters and pushes on the surrounding rock. It moves upward along cracks, and its gas bubbles can grow. Lava and fragments appear only after it reaches the surface; sometimes magma stops below. Increase the rock barriers and try again. Erupted material lands and cools, gradually leaving a mountain. Shields spread broadly, composite volcanoes collect lava and fragments, and an Omuroyama-style cone piles up scoria.

---

03 · Sea and lake water join the story
[Pause the lake case at 13%, 23%, 36% and 72%, tracing magma, fractures, the same rock layer and later water.]

Now look at a lake in a mountain. Do not add water yet! Follow the orange magma as it moves away. The heavy rock above remains. As support weakens, fractures grow and rock sinks along faults, leaving a basin. Rain and melting snow can collect later. If water leaks away, even a large basin may stay dry. Underwater volcanoes have another story: repeated deposits build up, and only some become islands.

---

04 · When the mountain is quiet
[Open How plants return, observe cooling, soil in cracks and seed arrival, then compare four environments at the endpoint. Replay 65–80% under thick ash to see vegetation decline.]

The eruption stops, but the mountain remains. Rock cools, cracks collect fine material and water, and wind or animals bring seeds. Are seeds enough to turn it green? Try a dry or cold environment and watch the seedlings. Fresh thick ash may bury them too. Omuroyama looks like a grassy hat today partly because people maintain its grassland through traditional burning. Grass, or its absence, cannot by itself tell us whether a volcano will erupt again.
