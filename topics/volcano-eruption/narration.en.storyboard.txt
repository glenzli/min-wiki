01 · Unit one: how magma reaches the surface
[Open Why does it erupt and pause to follow the same dike tip. Compare connection with a stalled intrusion. Move to Why do eruptions differ and observe a lava flow, fountains and an ash-rich eruption. The two questions have separate progress; their outcomes are not one measured eruption.]

Start with a question: why does magma reach the surface? Follow the same underground crack as magma moves upward. Sometimes the path connects and lava and fragments appear above ground. Sometimes magma stops below. Then ask why eruptions look different. Some lava spreads out, some rises in fountains, and some eruptions carry abundant ash. Bubbles, how readily magma flows, and its pathway all affect the result. Encountering water can change the process too. Follow the path, then compare the conditions.

02 · Unit two: how retained material builds land
[In How do mountains build up, follow the vent, flying fragments, darkening landings and retained deposits. Compare shield, composite and scoria-cone forms inside that question. Open How do mountains grow underwater to compare submerged growth and conditional emergence; an island is not an inevitable endpoint.]

Where does erupted material go? Lava spreads across the ground, fragments land on slopes, and cooled material stays. New deposits accumulate above it, gradually raising and widening the mountain. Broad shields, composite volcanoes and scoria cones record different deposits. Omuroyama is one scoria-cone example. Mountains also grow under the sea, where water changes pressure and cooling. Some emerge above the sea; others remain submerged. Mountain shape and land or sea are two different comparisons.

03 · Unit three: bare rock, grassland and lakes
[Begin ecology on the same already formed mountain. Follow fixed surface sites and retain progress while comparing four environments. Then open the separate lake question, observing basin formation before water balance. Explain that lakes can coexist with hydrothermal activity; neither question is a mandatory next step for one mountain.]

The mountain remains, but its surface can change. First follow how bare rock supports grass. Rock cools, fine material and water collect, and arriving seeds need a suitable place to root. Try dry, cold or thick-ash conditions at the same site. People’s traditional burning also maintains Omuroyama’s grassland today. Next ask where lake water comes from. A depression needs incoming water and a way to retain it. Grassland need not become a lake, and a lake does not prove that a volcano is permanently extinct.

04 · Unit four: how underground heat meets water
[Open Underground heat and water. Identify precipitation recharge, permeable pathways, the hot-rock zone and discharge, then follow the same water route. Details within the existing water route are illustrative and can continue automatically. Pausing the process stops only this question’s progress; hiding the scene or choosing reduced motion limits the detail movement. Give no uncalibrated depths, speeds or circulation ages, and do not place hydrothermal activity at the end of every volcanic history.]

Even without lava flowing above ground, rock below can remain hot. Rainwater enters cracks, gains heat from hot rock, and may rise through connected pathways to form a hot spring. Follow the water path separately from the magma path. Hot water can also flow from a lake bed. Not every hot spring comes from a volcano. Some water circulates deep enough to be warmed by hotter rock below. Whenever we see hot water, we can ask two questions: where did the water come from, and where did the heat come from?
