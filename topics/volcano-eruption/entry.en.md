## One: how magma reaches the surface

### 1. Why does it erupt? {#study-mechanism}

Follow the same dike from deeper supply to its advancing tip. First distinguish an underground intrusion from a pathway that has reached the surface. Activity below ground does not guarantee an eruption in this episode. [Open the eruption mechanism](entry.en.md#study-mechanism-en)

### 2. Why do some eruptions release flowing lava and others abundant ash? {#study-styles}

Compare how magma flows, how bubbles change and whether gas escapes. Supply, composition, temperature and external water also affect the outcome. Shields can erupt explosively too; mountain form alone cannot determine the style of an individual eruption. [Compare eruption differences](entry.en.md#study-styles-en)

## Two: how retained material builds land

### 3. How does a mountain accumulate? {#study-building}

Follow fragments ejected from a vent: they land, darken and remain while later deposits accumulate above them. Different distributions of lava and fragments can build broad shields, composite volcanoes and scoria cones. Omuroyama is one scoria-cone example; its mountain formed during eruption. [Observe mountain building](entry.en.md#study-building-en)

### 4. Can mountains grow under the sea? {#study-submarine}

Yes: material must accumulate there too. Seawater changes pressure, cooling and magma–water interaction. With insufficient supply, a mountain can remain submerged; only some emerge as islands. Land versus sea compares environments, while shield versus scoria cone compares forms. [Observe submarine growth](entry.en.md#study-submarine-en)

## Three: how the remaining surface changes

### 5. Why does some bare rock support grass while other ground stays bare? {#study-ecology}

Begin on the same already formed mountain, following cooling, accumulating substrate, seed arrival and rooting. Keep the same progress while comparing wet, dry, cold and thick-ash conditions at fixed sites. Traditional burning also maintains Omuroyama’s present grassland; that landscape is not a universal endpoint. [Compare bare ground and vegetation](entry.en.md#study-ecology-en)

### 6. Does a large depression inevitably become a lake? {#study-lake}

A depression needs incoming water and a way to retain it. In the collapse example, follow magma withdrawal and unstable rock sinking before water accumulation and leakage. Lake water is not cooled lava, and a lake does not establish permanent extinction: lake water can coexist with hydrothermal activity. [Observe basins and lake water](entry.en.md#study-lake-en)

## Four: how underground heat meets water

### 7. Why can hot water emerge without lava flowing out? {#study-heat}

Follow the heat source separately from the water source. Precipitation enters connected pores and fractures, gains heat from hot rock, and may return to the surface or a lake bed. Such systems occur in volcanic areas, but deep groundwater circulation can also produce non-volcanic hot springs. A hot spring alone does not establish magma beneath it. [Observe underground heat and water](entry.en.md#study-heat-en) ([NPS geothermal explanation](https://www.nps.gov/subjects/geology/hot-springs.htm); [USGS Yellowstone Lake hydrothermal study](https://www.usgs.gov/observatories/yvo/news/hydrothermal-system-yellowstone-lake))

How do volcanoes erupt, build land, and interact with plants and water?

Start with a question; you do not need to choose a named volcano first. Four units connect seven questions: how magma reaches the surface and why eruptions differ; how deposits build mountains on land and under the sea; how bare rock supports plants and how depressions retain lakes; and how underground heat warms water. Pause at a moment and follow the same dike, deposit, surface site or water pathway through different views. Land versus sea and shield, composite and scoria-cone forms are conditions compared within a question. Each question has its own teaching clock: one eruption, long-term accumulation, ecological establishment and groundwater circulation cannot share a conversion from progress to years.

## Connection and eruption differences: gas, flow and pathways

Magma originates through partial melting at depth; shallow storage is neither the origin of all magma nor an empty cavern. Recharge can change overpressure, while buoyancy, host-rock stress, tectonic extension, degassing and cooling also influence dike propagation. The mechanism question selects one recharge-driven episode: this episode produces surface ejecta only after the dike connects to the surface; a stalled intrusion remains underground. In the eruption comparison, falling pressure during ascent can let volatiles exsolve and bubbles expand. Flow properties and gas escape affect effusion, fountains and fragmentation. Composition, temperature, crystals, supply and external water also matter. Low viscosity does not rule out explosions, and mountain shape does not uniquely determine an eruption style. VEI primarily uses explosive ejecta volume together with plume evidence; it is not a scale for all energy, lava discharge or hazard. Bars and moving liquid textures are qualitative illustrations, not measurements of pressure, flow speed or eruption probability.

## Building land and changing surfaces: retained material at different scales

Lava and fragments accumulate during eruptions; extinction does not create the mountain. Repeated fluid lava flows can build a broad shield, lava and fragment deposits can build a composite volcano, and scoria deposited around a vent can build a scoria cone. Omuroyama is a scoria-cone example that also produced lava from its foot. Land and sea are eruption environments; shield, composite and scoria cone describe forms; grassland and lakes concern surface conditions and subsequent processes. These are different comparisons, not peer categories. Water pressure, cooling and magma–water interaction influence submarine products. Only some submarine volcanoes accumulate enough material to emerge as islands. The ecology question starts on the same already formed mountain: after cooling, weathered grains, windblown dust and organic material may build substrate. Seeds or spores must also arrive, with suitable moisture and temperature. Drought, cold, rapid drainage, thin soil, new lava or thick ash may delay or interrupt establishment. There is no universal species sequence, fixed number of years or inevitable forest endpoint. Traditional burning also maintains Omuroyama’s present grassland.

## Lake water and hot groundwater: basins, water balance and heat sources

In the selected collapse example, magma withdrawal changes storage pressure and support for overlying rock. Its weight remains, and unstable rock may subside along faults, creating a depression before water can collect. This requires no permanent underground cavern, and not every eruption causes collapse. Lake formation also depends on precipitation, runoff, evaporation and leakage. Volcanic lakes contain water; lava lakes contain molten rock. Lakes do not all wait for permanent extinction: lake water may coexist with later eruptions or hydrothermal activity, and Yellowstone Lake has hydrothermal discharge on its floor. The underground-heat question follows another pathway. Groundwater supplied by precipitation circulates through connected pores and fractures, gains heat from hot rock and may rise to discharge at the surface. In volcanic areas the heat source can include magma or still-hot intrusions. However, not every hot spring has a volcanic heat source: deep circulation can heat water in rocks that become warmer with depth. Gas emissions, hot springs, vegetation and lakes cannot individually establish the next eruption or permanent extinction. Dormancy and extinction assessments also need geological ages, tectonic setting, magma-supply history and monitoring evidence.

## A common misconception

There is no universal eruption–extinction–grassland–lake–hot-spring sequence. Land or sea, mountain form, vegetation, lakes and hydrothermal activity answer different questions and can occur in different combinations.

## Model limits

The seven connected questions are conceptual observations, not a measured continuous life history of one volcano. Each has an independent compressed teaching progress. One eruption, long-term deposition, ecological establishment, water balance and groundwater circulation have no common calibrated timescale. Cutaways, bed thicknesses, storage depths, clasts, soil and roots are scaled for recognition; lava textures, bubbles and moving water paths are illustrative too. Where hot melt or active water pathways are present, fine motion can continue automatically; pausing the process stops only the current question’s progress. Visibility and reduced-motion preferences limit detail motion, and cooling or a lack of water can leave no moving material. They do not provide measured depth, speed, temperature or eruption timing, and these scenarios are not hazard forecasts.

## Narration {#narration}

[Download spoken text](narration.en.txt?download) · [Download storyboard](narration.en.storyboard.txt?download)

### Unit one: how magma reaches the surface

Start with a question: why does magma reach the surface? Follow the same underground crack as magma moves upward. Sometimes the path connects and lava and fragments appear above ground. Sometimes magma stops below. Then ask why eruptions look different. Some lava spreads out, some rises in fountains, and some eruptions carry abundant ash. Bubbles, how readily magma flows, and its pathway all affect the result. Encountering water can change the process too. Follow the path, then compare the conditions.

*Scene cue: Open Why does it erupt and pause to follow the same dike tip. Compare connection with a stalled intrusion. Move to Why do eruptions differ and observe a lava flow, fountains and an ash-rich eruption. The two questions have separate progress; their outcomes are not one measured eruption.*

### Unit two: how retained material builds land

Where does erupted material go? Lava spreads across the ground, fragments land on slopes, and cooled material stays. New deposits accumulate above it, gradually raising and widening the mountain. Broad shields, composite volcanoes and scoria cones record different deposits. Omuroyama is one scoria-cone example. Mountains also grow under the sea, where water changes pressure and cooling. Some emerge above the sea; others remain submerged. Mountain shape and land or sea are two different comparisons.

*Scene cue: In How do mountains build up, follow the vent, flying fragments, darkening landings and retained deposits. Compare shield, composite and scoria-cone forms inside that question. Open How do mountains grow underwater to compare submerged growth and conditional emergence; an island is not an inevitable endpoint.*

### Unit three: bare rock, grassland and lakes

The mountain remains, but its surface can change. First follow how bare rock supports grass. Rock cools, fine material and water collect, and arriving seeds need a suitable place to root. Try dry, cold or thick-ash conditions at the same site. People’s traditional burning also maintains Omuroyama’s grassland today. Next ask where lake water comes from. A depression needs incoming water and a way to retain it. Grassland need not become a lake, and a lake does not prove that a volcano is permanently extinct.

*Scene cue: Begin ecology on the same already formed mountain. Follow fixed surface sites and retain progress while comparing four environments. Then open the separate lake question, observing basin formation before water balance. Explain that lakes can coexist with hydrothermal activity; neither question is a mandatory next step for one mountain.*

### Unit four: how underground heat meets water

Even without lava flowing above ground, rock below can remain hot. Rainwater enters cracks, gains heat from hot rock, and may rise through connected pathways to form a hot spring. Follow the water path separately from the magma path. Hot water can also flow from a lake bed. Not every hot spring comes from a volcano. Some water circulates deep enough to be warmed by hotter rock below. Whenever we see hot water, we can ask two questions: where did the water come from, and where did the heat come from?

*Scene cue: Open Underground heat and water. Identify precipitation recharge, permeable pathways, the hot-rock zone and discharge, then follow the same water route. Details within the existing water route are illustrative and can continue automatically. Pausing the process stops only this question’s progress; hiding the scene or choosing reduced motion limits the detail movement. Give no uncalibrated depths, speeds or circulation ages, and do not place hydrothermal activity at the end of every volcanic history.*
