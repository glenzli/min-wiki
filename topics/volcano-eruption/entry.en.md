Why do some volcanoes turn green, some stay bare, and some summits collapse into lake basins?

Choose the Omuroyama-style cone and open How plants return. Follow cooling, soil collecting in cracks and arriving seeds. At the same progress, compare wet, dry, cold and thick-ash conditions to see where seedlings establish. Then choose the volcanic lake: follow magma withdrawal, reduced support and sinking rock before water arrives. On land, also open Why does it erupt and change rock barriers to compare whether magma reaches the surface.

## From unrest to eruption: connection, gas and flow

Magma originates through partial melting at depth. Storage may contain melt, crystals and connected sills at several levels. Recharge can change overpressure, while buoyancy, host-rock stress, tectonic extension, degassing and cooling also affect dike propagation. This is one recharge-driven episode: discharge reduces overpressure after connection, while a stalled intrusion remains underground. The bars have no physical units and cannot predict eruption timing or probability. As magma rises, falling pressure allows dissolved volatiles to exsolve and gas bubbles to expand. Flow properties and gas escape influence effusion, fountains and fragmentation. Temperature, composition, crystals, magma supply and external water also matter; low viscosity does not rule out explosions. VEI primarily uses explosive ejecta volume together with plume evidence. It is not a score for total energy or hazard, nor a measure of lava discharge. Supply and rock barriers jointly determine whether the illustrative dike reaches the surface. Only then does the separate evolution of surface products begin.

## Different landscapes record different materials and histories

Repeated fluid lava flows can build a broad shield; lava and fragment deposits can form a composite volcano. Scoria cones grow from fragments deposited around a vent. Omuroyama also produced lava from its foot. Its cone formed during eruption, not as a consequence of extinction. Later weathering, erosion and vegetation modify the surface; traditional burning also maintains Omuroyama’s grassland. Submarine volcanoes likewise accumulate material, while pressure, cooling and magma–water interaction influence products. Only some accumulate enough material to become islands. Ecological recovery additionally needs suitable substrate, water and temperatures, along with arriving seeds or spores. Weathered grains, windblown dust and organic material accumulate. Drought, cold, thin soils, rapid drainage, new lava or thick ash can delay or interrupt recovery. There is no universal sequence of organisms, fixed timescale or inevitable forest endpoint.

## Lakes, quiet intervals and future activity are different questions

In the collapse example, magma withdrawal changes reservoir pressure and support for overlying rock. Its weight remains, and unstable rock can subside along faults. Fracturing and subsidence precede later lake filling; a permanent empty cavern is unnecessary, and not every eruption causes collapse. Craters and collapse calderas can provide water-retaining depressions. Lake formation depends on the water balance, permeability and other conditions. A volcanic lake contains water supplied later; a lava lake contains molten rock. A lake does not establish extinction: the volcanic center at Crater Lake retains future eruption potential. Dormancy commonly describes an interval between eruptions. An extinction assessment needs geological ages, tectonic setting and magma-supply history, and may be revised. Grass, water, absent emissions or a fixed number of quiet years cannot replace that evidence. An inactive old vent does not exclude new vents elsewhere in its field.

## A common misconception

There is no shared eruption–extinction–lake countdown. Grass and water do not establish extinction, and submarine volcanoes do not inevitably become islands.

## Model limits

These cases select particular conditions; they are neither surveyed reconstructions nor hazard forecasts. Landscape history and one eruption have independent timelines. Deposition, erosion, vegetation and water balance do not share a calibrated real timescale.

### Choose a volcano

Volcanoes have many shapes. A broad mountain, a round cone, a mountain under the sea and even a lake can tell a volcanic story. Choose one and open its cutaway. Follow magma to see whether it reaches the surface, then what remains.

*Scene cue: Show the five case cards, enter the Omuroyama-style cone and open the cutaway.*

### Erupted material stays

Look underground first. New magma enters and pushes on the surrounding rock. It moves upward along cracks, and its gas bubbles can grow. Lava and fragments appear only after it reaches the surface; sometimes magma stops below. Increase the rock barriers and try again. Erupted material lands and cools, gradually leaving a mountain. Shields spread broadly, composite volcanoes collect lava and fragments, and an Omuroyama-style cone piles up scoria.

*Scene cue: Advance deposition through cooling, compare the three shapes, then pause within the eruption detail.*

### Sea and lake water join the story

Now look at a lake in a mountain. Do not add water yet! Follow the orange magma as it moves away. The heavy rock above remains. As support weakens, fractures grow and rock sinks along faults, leaving a basin. Rain and melting snow can collect later. If water leaks away, even a large basin may stay dry. Underwater volcanoes have another story: repeated deposits build up, and only some become islands.

*Scene cue: Pause the lake case at 13%, 23%, 36% and 72%, tracing magma, fractures, the same rock layer and later water.*

### When the mountain is quiet

The eruption stops, but the mountain remains. Rock cools, cracks collect fine material and water, and wind or animals bring seeds. Are seeds enough to turn it green? Try a dry or cold environment and watch the seedlings. Fresh thick ash may bury them too. Omuroyama looks like a grassy hat today partly because people maintain its grassland through traditional burning. Grass, or its absence, cannot by itself tell us whether a volcano will erupt again.

*Scene cue: Open How plants return, observe cooling, soil in cracks and seed arrival, then compare four environments at the endpoint. Replay 65–80% under thick ash to see vegetation decline.*
