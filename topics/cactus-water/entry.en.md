Can a cactus survive drought because it never needs water?

Play a rain event: first rain reaches soil, then water travels through roots and storage cells fill out. Pause or scrub to compare shrinkage during drying. Enlarge the surface and observe gas exchange by day and night.

## A fleshy stem stores water in living tissue

Many desert cacti store water in cells and tissues of fleshy stems rather than a hollow bottle. After rain, uptake expands tissues and ribs permit changes in girth. Water use during drought reduces volume. Storage bridges dry intervals but is finite; root systems differ among species and environments.

## Timing gas exchange reduces water loss

Many desert cacti use CAM: carbon dioxide taken up at night is initially stored in organic acids and released internally for daytime photosynthesis. This can reduce daytime stomatal water loss. It does not mean respiration happens only at night; respiration and the timing of external carbon-dioxide uptake are different processes.

## Several traits conserve water without eliminating loss

A waxy cuticle reduces non-stomatal loss; reduced leaves or spines alter exposed surface, and spines can also shade and defend. Green stems perform much photosynthesis. These traits improve survival under dry conditions without making it unlimited. Not all succulents are cacti, and not all cacti inhabit hot deserts.

## A common misconception

Cacti still need water. A stem is not a hollow water bottle, and nighttime carbon-dioxide uptake does not mean respiration occurs only at night.

## Model limits

Storage cells, root paths and stomata are enlarged. Progress measures neither real days nor water use and is not watering advice. Day/night opening simplifies a typical CAM desert cactus and also depends on species and water stress. Gas symbols do not quantify exchange ratios.

### A green body in a dry place

Some cacti stay green through long dry periods. Does that mean they need no water? No. Many living cells in their fleshy stems store water, rather like distributing reserves among many small spaces. The stem is neither an empty bottle nor an endless supply.

*Scene cue: Show the storage view and the whole cactus.*

### After the rain

Make rain in the picture. Water taken up by roots gradually fills out the stem. Then let dry time pass. Pause and compare the stem and water store: are they shrinking? A cactus uses its reserves and eventually needs replenishment. Our buttons fast-forward a much slower real process. Enlarge the tissue and notice blue filling inside many cells. Rain has to reach the roots before storage rises; pressing a button does not fill a hollow bottle. Pause or scrub backward to inspect each part.

*Scene cue: Play rain, pause at the roots, then enlarge storage cells. In the surface view, follow brown carbon-dioxide markers inward and blue vapour markers outward.*

### Tiny doors by day and night

Look at the pores called stomata. Many desert cacti take in carbon dioxide mainly during cooler nights and keep pores more closed by day to save water. They initially store the carbon dioxide for later use. Life does not stop in daytime; different parts of the work are scheduled differently.

*Scene cue: Compare daytime and nighttime pores in the skin view.*

### Several features work together

A waxy surface helps retain water, while spines and body shape also affect exposure. Several features work together, and cactus species differ; not all live in hot deserts. When you next see one, inspect its ribs and green stem carefully without touching the sharp spines.

*Scene cue: Return to the whole plant and indicate ribs, skin and spines.*
