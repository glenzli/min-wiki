What do cells share, and how do different shapes support different jobs?

Explore structure, matter and energy, then specialisation: locate a cell's boundary and interior, compare respiration and photosynthesis, and observe skin, muscle, neurons, oxygen transport, defence and clotting. Each experiment retains its progress; changing specimens does not mean a cell transforms.

## Boundaries and internal spaces have different roles

The cell membrane separates inside from outside and regulates exchange. A plant cell wall lies outside it and provides mechanical support. The large leaf-cell vacuole has its own membrane and occupies much of the interior, with cytoplasm and organelles around it. Mitochondrial inner membranes fold inward, while chloroplasts contain a distinct thylakoid membrane system. Thickness, number and crowding are simplified.

## Genetic information does not require a nucleus

Most DNA in typical animal and plant cells is enclosed in a nucleus; mitochondria and chloroplasts also contain some DNA. Bacteria lack a nucleus but have DNA in a nucleoid region and carry out metabolism. Specialisation changes cell structures: mature mammalian red cells lack both a nucleus and mitochondria. A representative diagram is not a universal checklist. Skin barriers involve many cells. Skeletal muscle fibres usually have multiple nuclei, and contraction involves relative filament sliding, not shorter filaments. Neural signals propagate along membranes, with chemical transmitters crossing the illustrated synapse. Human platelets are cell fragments; clotting also uses plasma proteins. Oxygen transport, defence and clotting are different processes, not three mandatory steps for the same blood parcel.

## Making sugars and using nutrients can overlap

Aerobic respiration releases energy from nutrients, capturing some in usable chemical forms such as ATP and releasing some as heat. Sugar breakdown begins in the cytoplasm, with mitochondria involved in later steps. Leaf cells use light energy to make sugars using carbon dioxide and water. Sugar carbon comes from carbon dioxide; released oxygen comes from water. Plants respire in both day and night. Separate playback does not mean photosynthesis and respiration must take turns.

## A common misconception

Plants respire during the day as well as at night, and not every plant cell photosynthesizes. Bacteria have DNA and convert energy despite lacking nuclei and mitochondria.

## Model limits

These coloured, enlarged structures and pathways are not micrographs. Cells and membrane thicknesses do not share a common scale. Paths summarize multiple reactions rather than fixed pipes, and markers are not molecular ratios. Gold ATP is a symbol for molecules carrying usable energy. Green dots represent amino acids and a joined string represents a protein chain; ATP does not turn into these materials. This example only introduces energy support for synthesis, omitting ribosomes, RNA, amino acid activation and GTP-dependent steps. ATP yields, net gas exchange and anaerobic pathways are not modelled, and the two examples do not describe all bacteria. Specialisation views retain their own specimens and limitations: neutrophils do not represent every white cell, and the mitochondrial example of a typical nucleated cell cannot be imposed on mature human red cells. The six jobs are independent, not one simulated physiological timeline.

### What lies inside the boundary?

Let us look at a smaller world. Our bodies, leaves and bacteria all have something to do with cells. Choose the animal cell and find its outer cell membrane. It separates inside from outside and helps regulate what enters and leaves. Choose a structure and the lower window moves closer to that same place. The colours help us tell structures apart; they are not what our eyes would normally see.

*Scene cue: Choose the animal cell, then membrane and nucleus. Match the dashed whole-cell frame to the close-up.*

### Tiny bacteria have information too

Find DNA, which stores inherited information. In animal and leaf cells, most DNA is in the nucleus. Switch to a bacterium and pause. If that nucleus is missing, has the DNA vanished? No, it is still inside! A bacterium is itself a cell. The leaf cell also has a large vacuole, with green chloroplasts and other structures around it.

*Scene cue: Keep DNA selected across all three cells and pause. Select the bacterial nucleus to check the absence message, then inspect the leaf vacuole.*

### How an animal cell gets energy

A cell does more than hold things. Slowly move the progress control and follow the gold sugar. It first breaks down in the cytoplasm, and the smaller pieces keep moving. They and oxygen reach the circled mitochondrial region and take part in further reactions. Carbon dioxide and water form, and some energy from the sugar is captured in molecules called ATP. Follow the gold symbol, which represents ATP. Look at the green dots: each represents a small building block called an amino acid. The cell joins these building blocks into a protein chain. ATP supplies energy for some steps of this process; it does not turn into green dots. The energy did not appear from nowhere: it changed into a form the cell can use more easily.

*Scene cue: Choose the animal cell and mitochondrion. Scrub through sugar breakdown at 22–32%, follow the pieces and oxygen at 32–52%, ATP formation at 52–70%, then energy delivery and assembly through the end. Symbols are not molecular shapes or counts.*

### From leaf cells to specialised jobs

Now look at the leaf cell. Choose Making sugars with light. Light supplies energy; water and carbon dioxide help form sugars, and oxygen is released. Sugars can stay or supply other parts of the plant. Choose Using nutrients next. Plants also gain usable energy from sugars, during both day and night! We show these processes separately to look closely, although they do not have to wait their turn. Now compare jobs in the body: skin forms a barrier, muscle filaments slide, neurons carry signals and red cells transport oxygen. These are different cells, with shapes and structures suited to their jobs, not one cell transforming. Platelets are fragments made by large cells; they work with plasma proteins to help stop bleeding.

*Scene cue: Choose the leaf cell and chloroplasts to watch photosynthesis. Then select the mitochondrion and Using nutrients to compare. Then compare distinct specimens in the specialisation view, retaining their independent progress.*
