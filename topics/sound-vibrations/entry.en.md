How does a vibrating string become a sound we hear, and what do pitch, amplitude and a transmission medium each change?

Watch the source and fixed air parcel, then carry the pitch and amplitude comparison into the ear. Use a common time axis to separate frequency and amplitude, and compare a separate air/vacuum path. Each study retains its paused progress.

## Sound carries disturbances through a medium

A vibrating band creates pressure and density disturbances in nearby air. Airborne sound is longitudinal: mean parcel displacement is parallel to propagation, unlike transverse string motion. Dots represent average parcel positions, not individual molecular thermal trajectories. Energy propagates while parcels oscillate locally. A previously emitted wave keeps traveling after its source stops; such mechanical waves need a suitable medium. Solids can also carry mechanical waves; the separate vacuum comparison excludes solid connecting paths such as supports. Even the low setting remains taut. The speaking length between supports stays fixed; paired arrows encode relative tension. When the string is displaced, transverse components of tension provide restoring forces. Gold displacement and blue-gray force arrows encode different quantities.

## Frequency and amplitude differ

Frequency counts cycles per second and relates closely to the pitch of a simple tone. Amplitude measures displacement and relates to intensity. Perceived loudness also depends on frequency, distance and hearing. Motion is enlarged and slowed while the synthesized audio retains the model frequency, so visual cycle counts are not the audible frequency. In the ear, eardrum and ossicles pass mechanical vibration into the inner ear. Different frequencies favor different basilar-membrane regions. Inner hair cells produce receptor potentials and influence auditory-nerve firing through synapses; sound is not air flowing through a nerve.

## Conditions of the ideal-string model

For an ideal fixed-end string, f = (1/2L)√(T/μ), with length L, tension T and linear density μ. Quadrupling tension doubles frequency only with other quantities fixed. Real rubber changes length, thickness and material behavior as it stretches. The diagram fixes endpoints and linear density to isolate tension and does not calculate a real band's spectrum. Delayed copies of the same motion represent causal propagation through air. In the small-amplitude linear approximation, pressure variation Δp ≈ −B∂u/∂x; shading here shows relative compression and rarefaction, not calculated sound pressure.

## A common misconception

Louder need not mean higher pitched, and slow visual motion is not the actual audible frequency.

## Model limits

Visual time, displacement and propagation speed are not to physical scale. Air parcels omit thermal motion, 3D spreading and reflections. Separate audio is synthesized; pausing the diagram does not freeze real sound waves. Changing conditions restarts the experiment. Cochlear coordinates are qualitative pitch comparisons, not anatomical calibration for the synthesized 196–392 Hz tones. Study clocks are independent; displacement does not represent sound pressure or decibels.

### Change tension first

Increase only the tension and pluck again. Does the note sound higher? Pause and compare it with the previous setting. In this simplified experiment tension changes how quickly the band vibrates. A real rubber band also changes length and thickness, which can influence its sound together.

*Scene cue: Compare two tension settings with three seconds between plays.*

### Vibration becomes a message

Pause the second study at the cochlea. The eardrum moves the little bones, which pass changes into the inner ear. A different pitch shifts the highlighted region; changing amplitude alone need not shift it. Look at a sensory cell: bending its tiny bundle affects electrical activity in the cell and nerve. These are not air particles flying into the brain.

*Scene cue: Open the ear, pause at cochlear response, compare pitch and amplitude separately, then open the sensory-cell view.*

### Make the comparison fair

At the third stop, raise only pitch, then only amplitude. Within the same interval, does the curve gain cycles or grow taller? Suppose there is no air and no connecting table or string. The source still vibrates, but can sound reach the other side? Our vacuum path has no medium for transmission. Return to the first study to watch propagation take time.

*Scene cue: Compare presets in the same 20-millisecond window; switch air and vacuum, then revisit propagation delay.*
