How does one worker sense local information, and how does a colony live together without a commander?

Inspect W1's antennae, six legs and mandibles. Begin foraging and locate the first chemical markers. Compare a weakening cue with a physical blockage while tracking W1. Enter the same nest, then use a separate record for L1's egg, larval, pupal and adult stages.

## Anatomy connects sensing and action

Antennae detect chemicals and contact; three leg pairs attach to the thoracic portion of the mesosoma, while mandibles are mouthparts. The waist, gaster and exoskeleton serve different functions. These drawings distinguish structures rather than identify species. Separate observed contact or turning from inferences about the information an animal used.

## Trails and experience inform local choices

Research on Lasius niger examines chemical cues alongside route memory. Here fixed individuals mark locations only after actually returning through them. We distinguish weak signals from obstructed passage. Search paths, successful recall and the detour are finite teaching examples, not stochastic forecasts or guaranteed shortest routes.

## Development differs from colony organisation

L1's developmental record belongs to another individual, not adult W1 turning back into a larva. Workers care for young, maintain nests and forage; queens mainly reproduce rather than issue individual commands. Local encounters, brood needs and surroundings influence activity. Tasks, nests, reproductive systems and chemical communication differ among species.

## A common misconception

Hiding purple marks hides an explanatory overlay, not pheromone. A queen is not a commander, and an orderly trail is not proof of a universal shortest-path algorithm.

## Model limits

Four outdoor workers, one care worker and a queen are finite teaching characters. Paths, recruitment delay, successful memory, cue decay and detour success are authored. Chemical diffusion, wind, measured concentrations, stochastic search, energetics and optimal routing are not simulated. Purple and gold are explanatory overlays; hiding them does not change the model. Anatomy, nest cutaway and development have different illustrative space/time scales and are not measuring tools.

### Meet this worker

Find blue-marked W1. The two elbowed structures on its head are antennae, not legs. Count three legs on each side of the middle region: six altogether. Locate the mandibles by the mouth. We will follow this same worker outside.

*Scene cue: Select antennae, legs and mandibles, then open Find food.*

### Discover, then mark

At first there is no purple trail for this food. W1 searches and eventually finds it. On returning, it marks only places already passed. Purple makes an invisible chemical clue visible; real scent is not a string of beads. Nestmates then join this illustrative recruitment.

*Scene cue: Play to about40%, then compare marked and unmarked ground.*

### Different changes, different problems

Weak clues can lead to more searching; experienced W1 may recover a direction. A stone is different: scent cannot let an ant walk through it. Watch W1 explore above it and mark a new path on return. This is one possible success, not a guaranteed answer.

*Scene cue: Compare faster decay and blockage at the third station; inspect54%,75% and95%.*

### Another timescale inside

W1 brings food to the same nest. N1 is a different care worker, and L1 is a separate young individual. Review its egg, larval, pupal and new-adult record. Development takes far longer than a food trip. The queen lays eggs, rather than directing workers with a map.

*Scene cue: Enter the nest and finish the foraging journey; independently inspect all four L1 stages and a care visit.*
