## From air to inner-ear fluid

The pinna and canal guide sound toward the eardrum. Eardrum movement passes through the malleus, incus and stapes to the oval window, setting cochlear fluid and internal membranes in motion. Air and fluid have different acoustic impedances. Area differences and ossicular leverage improve energy transfer rather than creating energy. Compliance of the round window also allows fluid response; its detail is omitted to make the main pathway readable.

## A spatial clue to pitch

Width, stiffness and other mechanical properties vary along the basilar membrane. Sound produces a traveling wave whose response peak depends on frequency: higher frequencies favor the base, lower ones the apex. Each position responds over a range, and real sounds often contain multiple frequencies. Pitch selection moves the illustrated response envelope, while amplitude selection leaves that place unchanged, separating two concepts rather than measuring perceived loudness.

## Turning movement into an electrical response

Bundle deflection changes the opening of mechanically sensitive channels. Ion flow changes a hair cell’s receptor potential. Inner hair cells then alter transmitter release and auditory-nerve action potentials; a hair-cell receptor potential is not itself a nerve impulse. Outer hair cells contribute active amplification and sharp tuning. Brainstem relays and further brain processing are also needed; hearing is not performed by one cell alone.

## A common misconception

Sound is not a swarm of particles entering the ear, and air does not travel along nerves into the brain. Mechanical vibration becomes a cellular electrical response that then influences neural signaling.

## Model limits

The ear, uncoiled cochlea and single-cell view use different scales. Displacement, colors, cell counts and timing are teaching choices. Actual frequencies, outer-hair-cell feedback, the full tectorial membrane and all neural relays are not simulated. The close-up at B moves only after its local wavefront arrives; enlarged cell, bundle and fluid movements indicate causal links, not exactly matched phases in real tissue. This silent comparison does not test hearing, and the cell drawing is not a complete section of the organ of Corti. Zero amplitude means no input vibration. The diagram shows input-evoked responses, not spontaneous neural activity in quiet. Positive amplitude is not converted into hearing thresholds, firing rates or loudness.

### Receiving a gentle vibration

Have you heard wind in the leaves, or a little bird calling? When sound reaches an ear, the air is moving gently back and forth. Look at the outer ear and the little passage leading inside. At the end of that passage sits a thin eardrum. When the vibration reaches it, the eardrum moves too. We do not need to put anything into our ears. We can explore right here on the screen.

*Scene cue: Show the full ear at 0–23%; point to the eardrum at the end of the canal.*

### Three tiny helpers pass it on

Beside the eardrum are three very small bones. They are connected and pass the movement onward. Next it reaches the cochlea, curled up a little like a snail shell. Inside are fluid, delicate membranes and tiny cells. In our drawing, we can gently uncoil it to see its different places. Your real cochlea stays safely curled inside your ear.

*Scene cue: Move through the middle-ear stage into cochlear response; retain the whole-ear reference beside the uncoiled view.*

### Find the responding place

Let us pause and make a guess. Will a higher and a lower sound make the same place respond most strongly? Try the two pitch buttons and find where the pale gold shape moves. Take a little time to look. Different places in the cochlea are especially good at responding to different pitches. How high a sound is, and how loud it is, are two different things to think about.

*Scene cue: Pause at 62%, compare the two pitch choices, and allow an observation break. Do not play real sound.*

### A different kind of message

Now move closer to one sensory cell. It has a tiny hair-like bundle on top, but this is not like the hair on your head. A gentle bend affects how the cell sends its message. A nerve then carries a message toward the brain. More processing happens there, helping us experience sound. What a busy journey: first a vibration, then a message along a nerve.

*Scene cue: Choose the hair-cell view, move from 57% to 100%, then point to the purple neural route.*
