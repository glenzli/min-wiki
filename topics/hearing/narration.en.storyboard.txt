01 · Receiving a gentle vibration
[Show the full ear at 0–23%; point to the eardrum at the end of the canal.]

Have you heard wind in the leaves, or a little bird calling? When sound reaches an ear, the air is moving gently back and forth. Look at the outer ear and the little passage leading inside. At the end of that passage sits a thin eardrum. When the vibration reaches it, the eardrum moves too. We do not need to put anything into our ears. We can explore right here on the screen.

---

02 · Three tiny helpers pass it on
[Move through the middle-ear stage into cochlear response; retain the whole-ear reference beside the uncoiled view.]

Beside the eardrum are three very small bones. They are connected and pass the movement onward. Next it reaches the cochlea, curled up a little like a snail shell. Inside are fluid, delicate membranes and tiny cells. In our drawing, we can gently uncoil it to see its different places. Your real cochlea stays safely curled inside your ear.

---

03 · Find the responding place
[Pause at 62%, compare the two pitch choices, and allow an observation break. Do not play real sound.]

Let us pause and make a guess. Will a higher and a lower sound make the same place respond most strongly? Try the two pitch buttons and find where the pale gold shape moves. Take a little time to look. Different places in the cochlea are especially good at responding to different pitches. How high a sound is, and how loud it is, are two different things to think about.

---

04 · A different kind of message
[Choose the hair-cell view, move from 57% to 100%, then point to the purple neural route.]

Now move closer to one sensory cell. It has a tiny hair-like bundle on top, but this is not like the hair on your head. A gentle bend affects how the cell sends its message. A nerve then carries a message toward the brain. More processing happens there, helping us experience sound. What a busy journey: first a vibration, then a message along a nerve.
