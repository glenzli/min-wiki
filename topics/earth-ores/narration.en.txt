A pale line in rock can record a journey made by water carrying material. The fluid needs suitable ingredients, a fracture it can enter, and conditions that let minerals remain. Quartz can occur alongside metal minerals or with very little useful metal. Removing the picture's cover reveals relationships inside the same vein. We have changed how we look, rather than suddenly making the minerals grow.

Now follow an aluminum-bearing rock. Weathering and water let some components leave more easily while others remain relatively behind. No new aluminum-bearing material has appeared, yet it makes up a larger share of what remains. Bauxite usually mixes several minerals, and iron compounds often give it a red-brown color. Red soil alone does not tell us that the material is suitable for making aluminum.

Minerals must first separate from their parent rock before we compare individual grains in water. In this equal-representative-size exercise, selective transport moves quartz farther while denser cassiterite remains. Weak flow has not completed sorting; stronger transport can move both kinds beyond the window. Leaving the observation window does not mean disappearing. Real rivers also depend on grain size, shape and the bed.

Copper can enter wires, aluminum can enter packaging, and tin can enter solder. Finding their minerals does not mean that retrieving them is easy. We need more evidence and must compare content, recoverable amounts and costs, as well as land, water, energy and wastes. Using objects longer, repairing them and recycling continue the ore story into our own lives.
