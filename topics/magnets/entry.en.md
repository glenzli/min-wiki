A paper clip moves toward a magnet. Must a compass needle also point toward its center?

Compare materials and poles, then switch to the compass. Inspect the N tip on the left, above and on the right. Hold the probe position and flip the central magnet. Return to the object experiment and check its settings remain.

## Magnetic forces act before contact

A magnetic field can exert forces and torques on suitable materials or other magnets without contact. The diagram illustrates changes with distance, not a field that switches on at an invisible boundary. Visible movement also depends on friction and constraints, so no movement does not mean absolutely no magnetic response.

## Materials respond differently

Ferromagnetic materials such as iron can become strongly magnetized in an external field. Composition, structure and processing matter. Copper and aluminum have magnetic responses too, but are not normally picked up by an ordinary magnet in this static tabletop setup. Being metallic is not the same as being strongly magnetic.

## Poles and freedom to rotate

Bar magnets have north and south poles. Like-facing poles repel and unlike-facing poles attract. A freely rotating magnet may turn first and then approach with unlike poles facing. The tray constrains turning to isolate the comparison. Cutting an ordinary bar magnet gives smaller magnets with both poles, not isolated single poles. A separate compass study shows ideal point-dipole directions: the N tip varies with probe position and reverses when the magnet is flipped. Earth’s field and probe disturbance are omitted; this is not a near-field bar-magnet measurement.

## A common misconception

Magnets do not strongly attract every metal, and lack of motion does not prove zero magnetic response.

## Model limits

Motion is a qualitative, constrained demonstration, not a force measurement. For real play, adults should provide large enclosed magnets and keep swallowable magnets away from children.

### It moves before touching

Watch the paper clip. As the magnet comes closer, the clip can start moving before contact, almost as if an invisible hand were pulling it. The magnet’s field is acting on it. Approach slowly instead of pushing all the way at once, and find the part where movement becomes noticeable.

*Scene cue: Select iron, approach slowly and pause when movement begins.*

### Try other materials

Try copper, aluminum and wood with the same procedure. Predict first and give yourself time to notice the result. Copper and aluminum are metals, but do not visibly follow this ordinary magnet like the iron paper clip. Knowing that something is a metal does not settle how strongly a magnet attracts it.

*Scene cue: Select copper, aluminum and wood, pausing for two seconds each.*

### Meet another magnet

Bring in another magnet. Approach and see whether they join, then flip one. Pause: do they come together or move apart now? Changing the facing poles changes the result. This tray prevents unrestricted turning so we can see the difference between attraction and repulsion clearly.

*Scene cue: Choose magnet and compare before and after flipping, pausing three seconds.*

### Remember how to compare

The useful skill is changing one condition at a time: first material, then orientation. That helps us notice causes. Never put small magnets in your mouth or remove them from toys. Ask an adult to provide a suitable large magnet, explain your predictions and discoveries, and think of your next question together.

*Scene cue: Return to ready state and indicate material and flip controls.*
