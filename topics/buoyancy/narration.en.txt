Come to the water and look at this log, then the stone, coin and little boat. Lower them gently. Which will stay at the surface, and which will move down? The log floats and the stone sinks, but water pushes upward on both. Once the stone reaches the bottom, the bed helps support it too. Sinking does not mean there is no buoyancy. It means water cannot support all the weight. Next, we will use the water already in this space to find out how much support water provides.

Before adding an object, imagine drawing an outline in the water. Water is already inside. There is no membrane or little box. This parcel can stay here because the surrounding water supports exactly its weight. Now replace it with the object in the same place, keeping the outline and water level unchanged at first. The surrounding water still supports in the same way, but the weight inside has changed. Only now let go. Heavier than that water, it moves down; lighter, it moves up. The weight of the water excluded from this space tells us how large buoyancy is.

Follow the same object. Water pushes from all around, inward at every surface patch. Water itself has weight, so average pressure grows with depth. Together, these pushes leave an upward buoyant force. Now look closely at contact points A and B. Water molecules stay close together, keep moving and interact with their neighbours and the object. Many tiny effects together appear as pressure. Deeper down, it is average pressure that grows, rather than molecules moving faster or crowding closer together. Even when we pause this observation, real molecules keep moving.

Finally, follow this same steel. Its mass is seven hundred and eighty grams, but its solid volume is only one hundred millilitres. Water cannot support it even when fully submerged. Lift it out, spread it thinner, then slowly raise its edges into a boat. There is still the same amount of steel. It has not secretly become lighter. Return it to the water. While water stays outside, the dry cavity below the surface also keeps water out. The boat now displaces water heavy enough to support it, so it floats. Add a little cargo and it sits deeper. If water crosses the rim and floods the dry cavity, that space can no longer exclude as much water.
