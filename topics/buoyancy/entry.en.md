## Let water explain its support before replacing it

Imagine a parcel marked inside uniform still water, without a membrane or container. The parcel remains at rest, so the resultant of surrounding pressure must support its weight. Replace it with an object at the same location, initially retaining the water level, contact boundary and pose. The surrounding pressure forces remain unchanged; the weight inside the boundary changes. Buoyancy therefore equals the displaced water’s weight: F_b = ρ_water g V_displaced, where ρ_water is liquid density, g is gravitational acceleration and V_displaced is the underwater volume from which water is excluded. The imagined parcel does not act from afar: water touching the boundary exerts the force. Release only after replacement. A heavier object sinks and a lighter one rises; at freely floating equilibrium, buoyancy equals total weight. Sinking objects still experience buoyancy, with additional bed support only after actual contact. ## Connect whole-object pressure to molecular interactions

Still-water pressure acts normally inward on each surface patch. For approximately uniform density, gauge pressure is p − p₀ = ρ_water g h, where h is depth below the surface. The pressure gradient balances water’s weight, so pressure increases downward. The resultant surface force is upward and equals displaced-water weight. Local arrows and equal-area windows anchored at A and B on the same object show relative effects, omitting common atmospheric pressure; the uncalibrated drawing cannot supply numerical integration data. Liquid molecules remain close together, moving and interacting with neighbours. Their averaged effects produce pressure; a dilute gas picture of freely flying particles hitting walls is insufficient. This illustration keeps the same water temperature and approximately uniform density, without showing deeper molecules as visibly faster or more crowded. Molecular playback advances only the observation. The model uses representative fresh-water and seawater densities of 1.000 and 1.025 g/mL. At equal displaced volume, seawater provides slightly greater buoyancy; a freely floating object changes draft to regain balance. Moving a rigid, fully immersed object deeper leaves displacement and net buoyancy nearly unchanged.

## A common misconception

Sinking does not mean there is no buoyancy, and weight alone does not decide whether an object floats. Compare its weight with the weight of the water it excludes. Imagined replacement does not turn an object into water or create a separate parcel pushing from a distance. Making a boat removes no steel: it changes the underwater space the hull can keep free of water. Once flooded, the water-filled cavity cannot still count as displacement.

## Model limits

The model applies buoyancy relations and equilibrium tests in uniform, nearly incompressible still water. External handling lifts, spreads, bends and replaces the steel. Displayed positions and times are reversible teaching arrangements, rather than motion obtained from complete force equations. The log, stone and coin are not framed to a shared physical volume scale; the coin is enlarged. The retained steel mesh uses an internally consistent volume mapping to preserve material and link its hull profile to displacement. Waves, viscous flow, metal stress, capsizing and flooding rates are not solved. The 1400 mL rim capacity does not establish seaworthiness or a safe load. Bed contact assumes water still reaches beneath the body and does not model a seal. A/B close-ups retain the same pose and contact locations. Molecular sizes, spacing, neighbour links, paths and time are qualitative illustrations, without molecular-dynamics calculations. Pausing or ending that observation does not mean real molecules stop.

### At the shore: what floats and what sinks?

Come to the water and look at this log, then the stone, coin and little boat. Lower them gently. Which will stay at the surface, and which will move down? The log floats and the stone sinks, but water pushes upward on both. Once the stone reaches the bottom, the bed helps support it too. Sinking does not mean there is no buoyancy. It means water cannot support all the weight. Next, we will use the water already in this space to find out how much support water provides.

### Water supports water, then the object takes its place

Before adding an object, imagine drawing an outline in the water. Water is already inside. There is no membrane or little box. This parcel can stay here because the surrounding water supports exactly its weight. Now replace it with the object in the same place, keeping the outline and water level unchanged at first. The surrounding water still supports in the same way, but the weight inside has changed. Only now let go. Heavier than that water, it moves down; lighter, it moves up. The weight of the water excluded from this space tells us how large buoyancy is.

### Why does still water keep pushing?

Follow the same object. Water pushes from all around, inward at every surface patch. Water itself has weight, so average pressure grows with depth. Together, these pushes leave an upward buoyant force. Now look closely at contact points A and B. Water molecules stay close together, keep moving and interact with their neighbours and the object. Many tiny effects together appear as pressure. Deeper down, it is average pressure that grows, rather than molecules moving faster or crowding closer together. Even when we pause this observation, real molecules keep moving.

### The same steel becomes a hull that keeps water out

Finally, follow this same steel. Its mass is seven hundred and eighty grams, but its solid volume is only one hundred millilitres. Water cannot support it even when fully submerged. Lift it out, spread it thinner, then slowly raise its edges into a boat. There is still the same amount of steel. It has not secretly become lighter. Return it to the water. While water stays outside, the dry cavity below the surface also keeps water out. The boat now displaces water heavy enough to support it, so it floats. Add a little cargo and it sits deeper. If water crosses the rim and floods the dry cavity, that space can no longer exclude as much water.
