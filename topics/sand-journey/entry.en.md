## Sand describes size, not one substance

Sand is a grain-size category rather than a single mineral. Quartz is common in many continental sands because it resists weathering. Coastal sand may also include shells, coral skeletons or volcanic material. Sources can mix; color alone is not an identification. Microscopy and mineral analysis provide stronger evidence.

## Breakdown differs from transport

Weathering alters or breaks material where it lies; erosion and transport move it away. Flow, waves and collisions can wear grains and round their edges, depending on material and history. Shell fragments can form and move close to a coast, so not every beach grain has traveled from a mountain through a river. The diagram connects the rock source to a river while keeping the shell source near shore. Both preserve grain identity; shrinking and softened corners illustrate one possible abrasion history.

## A beach exchanges sediment

Waves, currents and wind sort grains by transportability, which depends partly on size and density. Changing conditions allow deposition, and later events can move grains again. A beach therefore has a changing sediment budget. Source interpretation requires surrounding rocks, biological material and transport directions, not simply the presence of sand.

## A common misconception

Sand is not always yellow quartz, and waves do not create every grain locally.

## Model limits

The gold ring and 16 marked fragments illustrate possible histories, not a route or rounding pattern shared by all sand. Time is compressed; particle mass, shed fines and actual transport forces are not calculated.

### Where a grain begins

Beach sand feels fine, but its grains may have different stories. Some were part of rocks; others came from hard shells. Choose a source and notice its color and shape. Today we follow one little grain, almost as if we had a magnifying glass to accompany it on part of its journey.

*Scene cue: Choose rock at 0% and locate the gold tracking ring on the rock.*

### From pieces to grains

Over long periods rocks can crack and change, leaving fragments. Water carries some away. Collisions and rubbing during the journey can make them smaller or rounder. Our picture moves quickly, but nature may take a long time. Pause and compare the grain in the circle with the one you saw at the beginning.

*Scene cue: Play to about 25%, pause and open the following view; scrub slowly to compare edges.*

### Try a different story

Now choose a shell and watch again. Shells can break too, and nearby currents can carry their fragments around. They need not visit a mountain before returning by river. One beach can contain grains from many places. Looking at color alone does not tell us exactly what every grain is made of.

*Scene cue: At 0%, choose shell and follow its coastal route without passing through the inland river.*

### A stop on the shore

When transport conditions change, some grains settle beside others to form the sand we see. This may only be a stop along the way: later waves or wind can move them again. When you examine sand with an adult, avoid throwing it or rubbing your eyes. Enjoy finding its many little colors and shapes.

*Scene cue: Hold at 100% with grains and ring remaining on the beach; scrub back along the same route.*
