Clouds already contain tiny droplets, so why do they not always rain? What if the air below is dry?

Compare the warm-cloud and ice routes, then lower humidity below the cloud and advance to the end. Compare drop radius and whether water reaches the ground.

## Droplets must grow

Rising air often cools through expansion, allowing vapor to condense on aerosol particles. Small droplets fall slowly and can remain within updrafts and turbulence. Condensation alone often does not produce large raindrops quickly. Rain also depends on growth, collision efficiency, cloud depth and residence time.

## Two common growth routes

In warm clouds, drops with different fall speeds can collide and merge. In mixed-phase clouds, ice grows by vapor deposition, aggregation and collecting supercooled drops; it may later melt into rain. Not all rain starts as snow, and not every collision merges droplets. Drop volume scales with the cube of radius.

## Rain may evaporate on the way

Below a cloud, drops exchange water and heat with the air. Dry air can increase evaporation; small drops may vanish before reaching the ground, producing virga. Temperature, fall distance, drop-size distribution and airflow also matter. Radar returns from falling particles need not match rainfall measured at the surface.

## A common misconception

A cloud is not a bag that leaks when full. Particles must grow, and falling water can still evaporate.

### Inside a cloud

A cloud already contains many little droplets and sometimes tiny ice crystals. They are much too small to immediately become the large drops landing on your umbrella. Today we magnify their story so our eyes can follow it. What might the little droplets need to do before they can more easily fall?

*Scene cue: Hold the cloud-droplet stage and pause for three seconds.*

### Growing larger

In one kind of cloud, some droplets meet other droplets and join into larger drops. Along another route, little ice crystals grow first and may melt into drops when they fall into warmer air. Both routes can help make rain. Watch each one and find the route that includes ice.

*Scene cue: Play the warm-cloud and ice routes in turn.*

### Not at the ground yet

The drops are falling, but their journey is not over. If the air below the cloud is dry, some water enters the air and the drops shrink. Pause, make the air below drier, then continue. Does the drop reach the ground, or does it disappear along the way?

*Scene cue: Lower humidity, pause halfway down and advance to the end.*

### Two different questions

Seeing clouds does not guarantee rain, and seeing streaks below a cloud does not guarantee rain at your feet. Droplets need to grow and complete the trip before the ground receives rain. You can notice clouds and ground conditions with an adult, but watch thunderstorms from the safety of a building.
