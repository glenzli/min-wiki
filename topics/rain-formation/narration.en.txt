A cloud already contains many little droplets and sometimes tiny ice crystals. They are much too small to immediately become the large drops landing on your umbrella. Today we magnify their story so our eyes can follow it. What might the little droplets need to do before they can more easily fall?

In one kind of cloud, some droplets meet other droplets and join into larger drops. Along another route, little ice crystals grow first and may melt into drops when they fall into warmer air. Both routes can help make rain. Watch each one and find the route that includes ice.

The drops are falling, but their journey is not over. If the air below the cloud is dry, some water enters the air and the drops shrink. Pause, make the air below drier, then continue. Does the drop reach the ground, or does it disappear along the way?

Seeing clouds does not guarantee rain, and seeing streaks below a cloud does not guarantee rain at your feet. Droplets need to grow and complete the trip before the ground receives rain. You can notice clouds and ground conditions with an adult, but watch thunderstorms from the safety of a building.
