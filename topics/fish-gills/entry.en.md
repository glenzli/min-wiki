Does a fish need to swallow bubbles to obtain oxygen from water?

First watch water pass the whole fish, then zoom into the same gill. Follow the yellow oxygen marker across the thin surface. Try less dissolved oxygen and slower renewal to see which dots or flow changes.

## Gills obtain dissolved oxygen

Oxygen molecules can be dissolved without visible bubbles. Across typical gill exchange surfaces, partial-pressure differences support oxygen transfer into blood and net carbon-dioxide transfer outward. Fish do not split water molecules to extract their oxygen atoms. Being surrounded by water therefore does not guarantee an adequate oxygen supply.

## Surface structure works with continuing flow

Filaments and many thin lamellae provide a large area and short diffusion distance. Blood carries oxygen away. In many fishes, opposing water and blood flow helps maintain an exchange gradient. Thin surfaces alone are insufficient: renewal of water and blood is essential to effective transfer.

## Ventilation varies among fish and conditions

This example represents an operculum-bearing fish. Other fishes may rely more on swimming to drive water over their gills. Temperature, oxygen availability, gill condition and metabolic demand all matter. Warmer water generally holds less dissolved oxygen, while demand may rise; water clarity or mouth movements alone cannot establish oxygen supply.

## A common misconception

Fish take up oxygen already dissolved in the water; they do not split water molecules or require visible bubbles.

## Model limits

The 20-second observation is not a molecular transit time. Two local channels illustrate opposing flow, not complete gill vasculature or a concentration-gradient model. Dots and arrows are not to molecular scale or measured flow speed.

### There is oxygen in water

We use lungs to obtain oxygen from air. How can fish obtain it while staying in water? Oxygen can dissolve in water without making visible bubbles. The coloured dots are symbols we have drawn so you can follow a little of that oxygen on its journey.

*Scene cue: Show the oxygen symbols on the water side.*

### Water passes thin surfaces

Water enters the mouth and passes over the gills. In the enlarged view, many thin surfaces contain tiny blood vessels. Together they provide a large area for gas exchange. Pause and point out the water side and the blood side, separated by a thin barrier.

*Scene cue: Use the zoom button to enter the same gill, then pause at the water and blood sides for three seconds.*

### Oxygen crosses a boundary

Follow the oxygen symbol. Some oxygen crosses the thin surface into blood, which carries it around the body. The fish does not pour a mouthful of water into its blood vessels or split water to make oxygen. Water flows past; oxygen crosses the exchange surface. Keep your eye on the same yellow marker as it crosses the boundary. It is not replaced by another marker on the far side. Water and blood move in opposite directions, an arrangement that helps gas exchange.

*Scene cue: Pause before, during and after crossing. Compare the external key while keeping the oxygen marker unobstructed.*

### Keep the water renewing

Water leaves near the gill cover and fresh water brings more oxygen. Some carbon dioxide made by the body also crosses into the water. Breathing requires continuing exchange, not a single action. Fish use different methods, and clear-looking water does not necessarily contain enough dissolved oxygen.

*Scene cue: Show outgoing water, compare low oxygen with slower renewal, then review the four steps. Do not infer real survival outcomes.*
