We use lungs to obtain oxygen from air. How can fish obtain it while staying in water? Oxygen can dissolve in water without making visible bubbles. The coloured dots are symbols we have drawn so you can follow a little of that oxygen on its journey.

Water enters the mouth and passes over the gills. In the enlarged view, many thin surfaces contain tiny blood vessels. Together they provide a large area for gas exchange. Pause and point out the water side and the blood side, separated by a thin barrier.

Follow the oxygen symbol. Some oxygen crosses the thin surface into blood, which carries it around the body. The fish does not pour a mouthful of water into its blood vessels or split water to make oxygen. Water flows past; oxygen crosses the exchange surface. Keep your eye on the same yellow marker as it crosses the boundary. It is not replaced by another marker on the far side. Water and blood move in opposite directions, an arrangement that helps gas exchange.

Water leaves near the gill cover and fresh water brings more oxygen. Some carbon dioxide made by the body also crosses into the water. Breathing requires continuing exchange, not a single action. Fish use different methods, and clear-looking water does not necessarily contain enough dissolved oxygen.
