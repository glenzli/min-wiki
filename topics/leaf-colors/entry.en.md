What does a leaf do from its bud to detachment, and why does its colour change?

Follow a young leaf unfolding on the same branch, pause at maturity and track water from roots. Enter the same leaf patch, cells and pigment compartments. Compare yellow and red routes, then inspect abscission and detachment. Leaf age, water progress and observation scale are separate.

## An expanding leaf connects the plant and air

This example starts with a leaf in a bud, not a seed. As it expands, xylem in its veins supplies mesophyll with water. Evaporation within leaves, water cohesion and water-potential gradients help maintain root-to-leaf transport; vapour diffuses through stomata. Stomata regulate exchange and water loss rather than remaining at a fixed opening. Light powers photosynthesis, while sugar carbon comes from carbon dioxide. Living plant cells also respire in day and night. The root-to-leaf diagram is structural, not a scale map of the branch.

## Resource recovery and pigment changes during senescence

Day length, temperature and plant condition influence senescence. Some photosynthetic machinery is dismantled and nutrients such as nitrogen and phosphorus can be remobilised. Chlorophyll loss reveals existing carotenoids; anthocyanins made by some plants accumulate in vacuoles, not reddening chloroplasts. Yellow and red compare capabilities rather than a universal sequence. Pigments coexist, and species and environment influence timing and appearance.

## Abscission, detachment and first-version limits

Changes near the stalk base weaken the connection; detachment leaves a scar on the branch. The leaf no longer receives root-supplied water through that connection. Continuous deformation illustrates expansion and detachment, with qualitative senescence and pigment states. Cell division, real wind-driven trajectories, cellular collapse after death and litter decomposition are not calculated. Evergreen plants replace leaves too, but multiple leaf ages may coexist rather than following one shared annual schedule.

## A common misconception

Not every leaf ends red: yellow need not turn red and chlorophyll does not become another pigment. Plants do not respire only at night, and evergreen does not mean an individual leaf lasts forever.

## Model limits

The same leaf persists through expansion, senescence and detachment; backward scrubbing revisits teaching states. Views have different magnifications. Shape changes, pigment trends, water markers and timing are qualitative, not species predictions. Microscopy represents expanded tissue before detachment, not bud development or postmortem collapse. Litter decomposition and ecosystem cycling are not simulated. The mature spatial model is 60 mm long and 200 µm thick. Blade, epidermis and tissue share one length unit and the camera zooms uniformly. These are teaching assumptions with schematic anatomy, not measurements of a particular species.

### Starting in a bud

Remember this branch. A small leaf unfolds from its bud. Its edge expands while its stalk stays at the same place. We are following one leaf, not turning four unrelated pictures. When it has expanded, move closer to discover its work.

*Scene cue: Move from the bud stop to maturity and follow the fixed branch and leaf edge.*

### A mature leaf at work

The leaf is connected to the whole plant. Water enters the roots and travels through xylem to the veins. Some evaporates inside the leaf and diffuses through stomata. A filled marker becoming hollow indicates a change of phase, not drops spraying from a pore. In light, mesophyll cells also use light energy to make sugars. Living cells respire during both day and night.

*Scene cue: Open water transport at maturity and pause inside the leaf; compare the main leaf, transport diagram and pore window.*

### Senescence and different colours

Move towards senescence. The tree recovers some nutrients, green chlorophyll decreases and existing yellow becomes easier to see. Some leaves also make anthocyanins that accumulate in vacuoles and give red colours. These are different routes; not every leaf goes from yellow to red. Enter the same patch and locate its pigments.

*Scene cue: Compare yellow and red, then tissue, cell, chloroplast and vacuole.*

### Leaving the branch is not rebirth

The connection between stalk and branch weakens and the leaf detaches. Its scar remains, and the same leaf is still visible. It no longer receives that root-supplied water flow. Real litter later dries, fragments and is used by decomposers; this model stops at detachment. Next year's leaf is new, not this old leaf turning back into a bud.

*Scene cue: Inspect detachment, the persistent scar and stopped water transport. Explain that scrubbing backward only revisits a state.*
