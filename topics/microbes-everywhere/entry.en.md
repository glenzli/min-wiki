If microbes arrive somewhere, must bacteria divide and viruses replicate?

Follow habitat E, reference cell B and comparison host H. Keep the chosen habitat as a reference, compare B's resource conditions, follow DNA and membrane changes, then test why mismatched surfaces or internal defense stop a virus.

## Widespread occurrence is not universal growth

Microbes are not one lineage: they include bacteria, archaea, microscopic fungi and many protists. Viruses are not cells but are studied in microbiology. Water, dust and contact transport microbes, while arrival, survival and reproduction require distinct conditions. Skin sites and soil pores contain different microhabitats. E shows representative residents, not identified species. Resource selection is a qualitative experiment for reference cell B, not a population growth estimate derived from the habitat illustration.

## Bacteria have cell machinery, but conditions still constrain division

B represents one thick-peptidoglycan-walled bacterium without an outer membrane. The wall supports shape, the membrane is selective, the nucleoid lacks a nuclear membrane, and ribosomes use RNA information to make proteins. Binary fission requires copying and partitioning genetic information and forming a division boundary, not cutting one genome in half. Real stages can overlap and growth varies with species and conditions. Early arrest in the resource-limited example is a teaching choice, not a universal stopping stage or a declaration of death. Yogurt, soil and gut examples are distinct communities whose roles depend on strains and context, not shape.

## Viral replication needs a suitable host; it is not cell division

Viruses lack their own ribosomes and cannot use external nutrients alone to make new viruses independently. The example explicitly switches to host H with an outer membrane and a thin wall, referencing a T4-like phage and an E. coli-like host, not thick-walled B. Surface compatibility is one attachment condition; internal defense can still block replication. The continuous model retains the original DNA, assembly parts and offspring identities. The host opens before offspring leave. This is one lytic route, not a claim that all viruses lyse cells, kill their hosts or infect people.

## A common misconception

Finding microbes does not prove active reproduction. Bacteria are not viruses, and a similar-looking cell or available nutrients do not guarantee infection and replication.

## Model limits

E is a habitat illustration; B and H are explicitly different reference objects, not one cell identified from E. Colours, scale, counts, layer spacing and time are illustrative. The resource comparison has no universal real growth rate and cannot guide culturing, identification or food, water or health-safety judgments.

### What is missing after arrival?

Tiny residents may occur between soil grains, in pond water and on skin. Air can carry spores or bacteria-bearing droplets too. Reaching a new place does not establish survival, let alone making more. Different habitats suit different residents. These illustrations are not photographs of newly collected samples. Keep one habitat beside the observation workspace.

*Scene cue: Compare the four habitats and zoom within the same illustration; retain one habitat as the reference.*

### Borrow a reference cell to inspect its tools

Now switch to reference cell B. It is not a species identified in the earlier picture; it is an example for studying structure. A wall supports it, and an inner membrane regulates selected exchanges. Purple lines represent DNA without a nuclear membrane. Ribosomes make proteins from information. A virus has no ribosomes of its own.

*Scene cue: Inspect wall, membrane, DNA and ribosomes; play the selected membrane routes once.*

### Conditions support the next step

Follow one growth sequence. The cell adds material, copies and separates genetic information, then forms a partition and becomes two daughter cells. One genome is not simply cut in half. In the resource-limited comparison, the sequence stops early. This means conditions are insufficient in this example, not that all bacteria stop at that stage or that this cell has died.

*Scene cue: Run growth-supporting and limited conditions to their endpoints; return to verify separately retained progress.*

### A virus needs another gate

Finally, switch explicitly to host H. Its outer membrane and thinner wall differ from B. This phage must encounter a suitable cell before its DNA can enter and use cellular conditions to make parts. A mismatched surface blocks entry; effective internal defense blocks offspring even after entry. In the successful pathway, parts assemble and the boundary opens before new phages escape. Arrival, bacterial growth and viral replication are not the same thing.

*Scene cue: Compare all three host cases; pause during entry, assembly and release, then return to the habitat.*
