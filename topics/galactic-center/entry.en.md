Does a star beside a black hole necessarily disappear at once?

Compare detached, overflow, and wind scenarios. Enable binary motion, locate the common center, and follow which gas stays and which escapes.

## A binary, not a galactic nucleus

This lesson concerns a stellar-mass black hole and a companion star, not a reconstruction of the Milky Way’s central supermassive black hole. In an approximately isolated binary, both bodies orbit their common center of mass, with M₁r₁=M₂r₂. The heavier body traces the smaller orbit. Radius, separation, and wind determine mass supply; a nearby black hole alone does not guarantee sustained accretion.

## Two supply mechanisms

The corotating effective potential combines gravity and centrifugal terms. A star close to filling its Roche lobe can transfer gas near the inner Lagrange point. The circular guide shows an equivalent-volume radius, not the lobe’s true equipotential shape. Wind first flows outward, with some subsequently captured. Mechanisms can mix; Cygnus X-1 is a real system used to study wind-fed accretion.

## Emission and inflow require more physics

Gas carries angular momentum, so it need not fall radially inward. Disk formation and further accretion require dissipation and angular-momentum transport. Released gravitational binding energy can become heat and radiation; X-ray spectra and variability probe this process. Emission is outside the horizon, and supply need not instantly equal inner accretion. A visible companion here does not mean a real star never loses mass.

## A common misconception

Steady transfer of outer gas differs from destroying a whole star in one tidal encounter. A Roche lobe is not an event horizon.

## Model limits

A fixed-mass circular binary and constructed continuous gas paths illustrate supply, without magnetohydrodynamics or real mass-loss evolution. The enlarged inner view includes simplified light bending, not a complete numerical-relativity simulation.

### Two traveling partners

Here are two space partners: a bright star and a black hole. Watch them move together. The black hole does not always sit still while only the star goes around it. Both move around a shared center. And the star that looks so large does not have to disappear immediately just because a black hole is nearby.

*Scene cue: Choose detached and enable binary motion.*

### Outer gas leaves

Try a different situation. The star’s outer layers reach farther out, and some gas can leave from the side near the black hole. Pause and look: is the star still there? Give yourself a moment, then follow the bright marker. Only some outer material has left. The star we saw before remains.

*Scene cue: Select overflow and pause four seconds to locate the surviving companion.*

### A curving stream

The gas was already moving, so it does not all drop along one straight line. It follows a curved route and keeps circling near the black hole. Hot gas nearby can shine. Try the close view, then pull back to the whole system and connect the stream’s starting point with the place it is going.

*Scene cue: Follow the marker; compare close and overview views.*

### A wind from a star

A star can also send gas outward as a stellar wind. This is not the air we breathe. The black hole catches only some of it; other gas keeps traveling away. Compare the three situations: little supply, overflow from one side, and wind spreading outward. They have the same two partners, but different stories.

*Scene cue: Choose wind and locate captured and escaping gas.*
