01 · Open Earth and travel to its center
[Drag Earth’s close view, then open the whole-world cutaway. Pause in each layer and reach the center; compare liquid outer core and solid inner core.]

Gently drag the picture to see the same terrain from another direction. Then open a cut through the whole Earth for an imaginary journey. First comes thin rocky crust, under both land and ocean. Next is the thick mantle: hot rock that is mostly solid, changing shape only over very long times. Farther in, the outer core is flowing metal. At the exact center, the inner core is solid again, under enormous pressure. Pause and find the layers we passed. Seawater stays near the exterior; it does not reach the center.

02 · Different worlds, different interiors
[Compare Mercury, Venus and Mars core labels, then rotate Jupiter and Neptune cutaways and pause at their mixed central regions.]

Mercury has a thin rocky exterior and a large metallic interior. Venus is very hot, but scientists still investigate which parts of its core can flow. Marsquakes offer new clues that a solid inner core may lie at the center of Mars. Now try Jupiter: its stripes are clouds, not floors. Material becomes denser inward, and heavier ingredients may mix with their surroundings instead of making a neatly edged fruit stone. Neptune is not wrapped in an ice rink either. Worlds drawn as similar balls can be very different inside.

03 · Titan’s lakes and ice
[Drag the representative lakeshore view. In the cutaway, follow the ice shell, candidate high-pressure ice and rocky core, pointing out uncertainty labels.]

Titan travels around Saturn: it is a moon. Its lakes contain methane and ethane, not water. Water is frozen into hard ice here. Lakes occur in some places rather than wrapping the whole moon. What is below the ice? Scientists once inferred a large buried water ocean. New clues suggest another possibility: high-pressure ice near melting, with pockets of liquid water. We show a possible answer while keeping the question mark. Farther inward is a region that may be rich in rock.

04 · A hot distant world, and what remains unknown
[Choose 55 Cancri e, inspect each candidate label and reach the center. Return to the close view with the illustration and unknown magma-floor caveats visible.]

Finally visit fifty-five Cancri e. It is so close to its star that rock on the star-facing side may become hot enough to flow. Nobody has photographed its shore. Our picture can continue to the center, but its layers are one possible answer. How deep the magma reaches and what a metal core might be like are still unknown. Scientists listen to seismic clues and read light, gravity and magnetic fields. The interesting question is not only where we went, but how we know what might be there.
