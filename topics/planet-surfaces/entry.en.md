## Whole-world cutaways: crust, mantle and core are different

Mercury, Venus, Earth and Mars are rocky planets with different proportions and states of rocky and metallic material. Mercury has a large metal core, with MESSENGER gravity and rotation data supporting a solid inner core. Venus is expected to have a rocky mantle and iron-rich core, but separate solid and liquid core regions are not established. Earth’s mantle is mostly solid rock that can creep over geological times; it is not a global magma ocean. A liquid iron-rich outer core surrounds a solid inner core, showing that phase depends on pressure and composition as well as temperature.

InSight provides seismic constraints on Mars. Bi et al. (2025) reported evidence for a solid inner core about 600 km in radius, updating earlier core models. Dimensions, composition and core-overlying transitions still require testing. Its inner-core region is labeled as a new research interpretation rather than an interior photograph.

The whole-world section reaches the exact center. Colors distinguish materials and thin crusts are enlarged. Earth’s oceans occupy surface basins and Titan’s hydrocarbon lakes are local liquids; neither becomes a thick global interior shell. A close view explains terrain and liquid surfaces, while the cutaway explains internal regions.

## Giants and icy moons: mixed boundaries and changing evidence

Jupiter’s clouds are not a solid surface. Deeper hydrogen-helium material becomes dense, with conducting metallic hydrogen expected at high pressure. Juno gravity data support models with a dilute core: heavy material is mixed outward rather than necessarily confined to a sharply outlined solid kernel. Neptune’s ice-giant label does not establish a walkable ice shell or pure-water sea. Gravity, magnetism and material models constrain its high-pressure mixtures, but a 2025 study still permits both water-rich and rock-rich interiors. Drawn boundaries need not be sharp physical interfaces.

Titan is Saturn’s moon. Cassini observed mainly polar methane-ethane lakes, while water ice forms the exterior. Those surface liquids are separate from possible deep liquid water. Petricca et al. (2025) reanalyzed the tidal gravity response and found strong dissipation difficult to reconcile with a global subsurface ocean, favoring near-melting high-pressure ice with possible water pockets. This view adopts that candidate interpretation with uncertainty labels rather than treating either an ocean or the new model as directly seen.

## Exoplanet limits: reaching the drawn center is not measuring it

55 Cancri e is a rocky exoplanet very close to its star. Webb thermal emission and spectra constrain a possible atmosphere and extreme surface heat, motivating a dayside silicate magma ocean. This is not a photographed coast or measured sea floor. Mass, radius and spectra do not uniquely fix deep composition or phase. The hot exterior, silicate interior and candidate metal core are one explanatory arrangement, all marked uncertain. The outer color band is not a uniform global molten shell, and its bottom is not a measured magma-ocean boundary.

Earth and Mars can be studied seismically; gravity, magnetism, rotation, tides, spectra and high-pressure experiments also constrain worlds. These support interior models rather than transparent photographs. Dragging changes the viewing angle. Moving through layers explains material order, not a survivable spacecraft trajectory or measured thicknesses.

## A common misconception

Cloud tops are not ground; hot material is not necessarily all liquid; reaching the drawn center does not mean its structure has been directly seen. Hydrocarbon lakes, seawater and molten rock are different liquids.

## Model limits

Worlds have equal display sizes. Terrain and 3D interiors are procedural illustrations, not measured maps or transparent photographs. Layer thickness, color, travel speed and core size are uncalibrated. Jupiter and Neptune may have gradual transitions. Venus’s core, Mars’s new inner-core interpretation, Titan’s depths and 55 Cancri e retain their specific evidence limits. A candidate model can reach the center without being a measured travel path.

### Open Earth and travel to its center

Gently drag the picture to see the same terrain from another direction. Then open a cut through the whole Earth for an imaginary journey. First comes thin rocky crust, under both land and ocean. Next is the thick mantle: hot rock that is mostly solid, changing shape only over very long times. Farther in, the outer core is flowing metal. At the exact center, the inner core is solid again, under enormous pressure. Pause and find the layers we passed. Seawater stays near the exterior; it does not reach the center.

*Scene cue: Drag Earth’s close view, then open the whole-world cutaway. Pause in each layer and reach the center; compare liquid outer core and solid inner core.*

### Different worlds, different interiors

Mercury has a thin rocky exterior and a large metallic interior. Venus is very hot, but scientists still investigate which parts of its core can flow. Marsquakes offer new clues that a solid inner core may lie at the center of Mars. Now try Jupiter: its stripes are clouds, not floors. Material becomes denser inward, and heavier ingredients may mix with their surroundings instead of making a neatly edged fruit stone. Neptune is not wrapped in an ice rink either. Worlds drawn as similar balls can be very different inside.

*Scene cue: Compare Mercury, Venus and Mars core labels, then rotate Jupiter and Neptune cutaways and pause at their mixed central regions.*

### Titan’s lakes and ice

Titan travels around Saturn: it is a moon. Its lakes contain methane and ethane, not water. Water is frozen into hard ice here. Lakes occur in some places rather than wrapping the whole moon. What is below the ice? Scientists once inferred a large buried water ocean. New clues suggest another possibility: high-pressure ice near melting, with pockets of liquid water. We show a possible answer while keeping the question mark. Farther inward is a region that may be rich in rock.

*Scene cue: Drag the representative lakeshore view. In the cutaway, follow the ice shell, candidate high-pressure ice and rocky core, pointing out uncertainty labels.*

### A hot distant world, and what remains unknown

Finally visit fifty-five Cancri e. It is so close to its star that rock on the star-facing side may become hot enough to flow. Nobody has photographed its shore. Our picture can continue to the center, but its layers are one possible answer. How deep the magma reaches and what a metal core might be like are still unknown. Scientists listen to seismic clues and read light, gravity and magnetic fields. The interesting question is not only where we went, but how we know what might be there.
