## Forces move air; temperature labels alone do not

The horizontal pressure-gradient acceleration points toward lower pressure at the same height: aₚ = −∇ₕp / ρ. Differential heating changes the thickness and mass distribution of air columns; adjustment can establish a local circulation. Temperature alone does not determine surface pressure. Real winds also depend on Earth’s rotation, friction and prior motion. This illustration includes only a local circulation maintained by differential heating. Equal heating does not mean that real-world winds must stop.

## How terrain and the day–night cycle change wind

Solid boundaries limit flow through them. Terrain, surface friction and the incoming flow shape the resulting wind field. Separation and turbulence can occur on the lee side; hills do not all create the same eddies. These smooth, prescribed paths do not calculate building wind loads, simulate turbulence or predict local wind speed. Sea and land breezes are thermally driven local circulations. Differences in effective heat capacity, mixing and evaporation produce a daily temperature contrast; the pressure field changes as the air columns adjust. Background winds, clouds, terrain and sea temperature can weaken, strengthen or prevent this circulation. The arrows show idealized established circulations, not an instantaneous reversal of all winds at sunset.

## Storms and object responses require more than wind strength

Typhoon is a regional name for a tropical cyclone, involving large-scale ocean–atmosphere energy exchange and rotating circulation. A tornado is a strongly rotating column of air connected to a convective cloud and in contact with the ground. Tornado formation has multiple pathways; this project illustrates one associated with supercells. Tropical-cyclone rainbands can sometimes spawn tornadoes, but that does not make the two phenomena identical. Air transfers momentum through pressure and friction. With other conditions approximately fixed, drag F ≈ ½ρCᴅAv². Actual responses also depend on shape, exposed area, anchoring and material. Sand has an entrainment threshold; seeds respond to both gravity and drag. These dimensionless responses do not convert the slider into wind categories, electricity output or safety thresholds. The scale view uses one kilometres-to-pixels conversion for the playground, city area and cloud envelope in every frame. The 100 m and 20 km references are defined examples; 500 and 1000 km are illustrative cloud-system spans. The location is imaginary and the dashed cloud boundary is not a wind-speed contour. The central locator ring aids readability and does not represent the playground’s physical size.

## A common misconception

Coastal breezes can be understood through an idealized circulation, but not every wind follows a closed loop. Tropical cyclones exchange air with their surroundings, and a tornado is not a miniature tropical cyclone. Typhoons and hurricanes are regional names for the same kind of storm; cloud size does not directly give the strong-wind extent.

## Model limits

This project prescribes teaching paths and dimensionless responses rather than solving full weather equations. Equal heating stops only the circulation maintained by heating differences in this example, not all real-world wind.

### Air begins moving

Hold out your hand. You may feel a breeze even though you cannot see air. Wind is moving air. Unequal heating can redistribute air and create pressure differences that push it. Watch air rise on one side, return above and flow back near the ground.

*Scene cue: Select Coast and land, start observation, compare clear day and night, then move the heating contrast to the middle.*

### Take the clues to the next stop

Remember three questions: where does air come from, where does it rise, and where does it go aloft? At the coast, air forms a circulation. In a tropical cyclone, air also flows in below, rises and flows out above, while rotating around a center. It needs a warm sea, moisture and suitable surroundings. It is not simply a larger version of the coastal breeze.

*Scene cue: Select each colored route segment, then move to the tropical cyclone. Compare the side route and use Look down at the rotation.*

### How big is the storm?

Typhoons and hurricanes are names for the same kind of storm in different ocean regions. Start with this playground, one hundred metres long. Zoom out and it becomes a dot in a city area. Zoom out again and even the city area looks small. This example cloud system is five hundred kilometres wide: five thousand playground lengths end to end! Cloud extent is not strong-wind extent, and winds vary beneath the clouds.

*Scene cue: Move from playground to city area to whole storm. At 500 km, point out the 20 km city square and the common ruler. Select 1000 km and watch the cloud diameter double.*

### What wind does

Take the same three questions to a tornado: air can enter a near-ground vortex, spiral upward and connect to a thunderstorm. Formation also depends on winds at different heights, updrafts and conditions near the ground. It is not a small tropical cyclone. Back at the coast, watch one wind move plants, seeds, a sail and a wheel. During storms, we follow adults and local warning guidance to shelter.

*Scene cue: Compare the tornado route and ground-contact conditions, then return to the coast to observe object responses.*
