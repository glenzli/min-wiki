Hold out your hand. You may feel a breeze even though you cannot see air. Wind is moving air. Unequal heating can redistribute air and create pressure differences that push it. Watch air rise on one side, return above and flow back near the ground.

Remember three questions: where does air come from, where does it rise, and where does it go aloft? At the coast, air forms a circulation. In a tropical cyclone, air also flows in below, rises and flows out above, while rotating around a center. It needs a warm sea, moisture and suitable surroundings. It is not simply a larger version of the coastal breeze.

Typhoons and hurricanes are names for the same kind of storm in different ocean regions. Start with this playground, one hundred metres long. Zoom out and it becomes a dot in a city area. Zoom out again and even the city area looks small. This example cloud system is five hundred kilometres wide: five thousand playground lengths end to end! Cloud extent is not strong-wind extent, and winds vary beneath the clouds.

Take the same three questions to a tornado: air can enter a near-ground vortex, spiral upward and connect to a thunderstorm. Formation also depends on winds at different heights, updrafts and conditions near the ground. It is not a small tropical cyclone. Back at the coast, watch one wind move plants, seeds, a sail and a wheel. During storms, we follow adults and local warning guidance to shelter.
