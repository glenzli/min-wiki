01 · No forests, but an interdependent world
[Inspect functional links on the representative microbial shore, then compare the broad oxygenation interval. This is not one rock's continuous movie or the extinction of all anaerobes.]

Long ago, a shore without trees or animals could still contain an interdependent microbial world. Some microbes made organic matter; others used and decomposed it. Oxygen later accumulated and changed environments, yet low-oxygen habitats continued to support many microbes.

02 · Look at bodies and relationships
[Switch body and trace evidence in Ediacaran and Burgess scenes. Compare the end-Permian and end-Cretaceous aftermath, identifying food-web links rather than ancestry.]

A body impression, a set of scratches and a chemical signal provide different clues. Ancient seas held bottom dwellers, filter feeders and swimming predators. Environmental crises ended some branches while others continued. Continuing does not mean every original member survived.

04 · Approach a different kind of tree
[Rotate the three plant forms and identify cushions, nodes and fronds. Separate fossil constraints, comparisons and artistic completion without calling the procedural 3D a scan.]

Some ancient trees left diamond-shaped leaf cushions. Others connected branches at repeated nodes. Some had fern-like leaves but belonged to seed-producing plants. Decomposers worked in these forests too. Only some organic matter remained buried under suitable conditions and could later become coal.
