If seawater cools a lava surface, how can an underwater volcano keep growing or even build an island?

## Flow and crust formation shape the lava

Pillow lavas form bulbous or tubular lobes. Relatively slow extrusion allows a rind to develop while internal passages can feed hotter lava into new lobes. Larger flows can instead form sheets and other shapes. A visible crust does not mean the whole interior has solidified, and pillows are not permanently hollow bags. Our deep-water scene selects one slow-flowing example rather than all submarine lava forms. Wrinkles and cracks help distinguish new and older lava. A few illustrative cutaways reveal interiors; they are not natural hollow openings.

## Pressure, gas and contact with water all matter

Ambient pressure limits gas expansion, but gas content, magma supply and mixing also influence eruption behavior. Rapid heat transfer between magma and water can cause violent fragmentation. Explosions driven mainly by magmatic gases were directly observed at NW Rota-1 at about 550–560 meters depth. A single depth cannot divide quiet deep eruptions from explosive shallow ones, and seawater does not universally shut off deeper magma supply. Sunlight is weak or absent in the deep ocean, so this example uses vehicle lighting. Mixing hydrothermal fluid with cold seawater can create suspended mineral particles. This turbidity differs from mist made of condensed droplets above the sea; neither should simply be treated as steam bubbles.

## Emergence depends on accumulation and loss

A volcanic island does not rise as one cone that inflates uniformly. Erupted material follows the existing terrain, then cools, fragments or slides into bounded deposits. Low-rate pillow and tubular lavas can build a deep base; broader lobate and sheet flows fill lows and widen the edifice; shallow fragments can alternate with later lava; subaerial flows may cap the emerged summit. Erosion, landslides, subsidence and sea-level change also matter. This fixed-sea-level illustration compares continuing supply with an early stop, not two numerical forecasts.

## A common misconception

Seawater is not a universal fire extinguisher, and lava is not an ordinary burning flame. Deep water does not guarantee no explosions, shallow water does not guarantee explosions, and an underwater volcano need not become an island.

## Model limits

These are not inevitable stages of every volcano. There is no real depth scale, and compressed island-building time differs from the cooling demonstration. Geometry, glow, lobe counts, fragment paths and erosion are qualitative teaching choices. The model does not solve real fluid flow, heat transfer, fragmentation, landslides or tsunamis, or predict an island's formation time. Strata, cracks, light attenuation, particle density and surf are deterministic illustrations, not geological surveys, optical measurements or flow solutions. Fading plumes end this teaching segment; real hydrothermal activity need not stop with the eruption.

### Look beneath the sea

Look, a mountain is hiding under the sea. Down here the vent has no sunlight, so a little lamp on the underwater vehicle helps us see. Magma that comes out is called lava. Seawater cools the outside quickly, while the inside may stay hot. Press Run and move closer. Can you find the dark skin and tiny glowing seams?

*Scene cue: Run deep water from zero to about 25%. Point to the vehicle lamp, then choose the vent close-up and compare solid skins, advancing seams and the few illustrative cutaways.*

### One lobe after another

Do those rounded shapes look like pillows? They are not soft, and we must never touch them. Hot lava inside can keep pushing out, making another lobe. When the inside cools too, it becomes rock. Lava under the sea can have many shapes. This is just one example.

*Scene cue: Keep the close-up and run from 25% to the end. Compare new growth, crust formation and the fading orange interior.*

### Water meets magma

Now look at the example near the surface. When water meets very hot magma, violent changes can sometimes scatter fragments. Tiny particles make water cloudy. The white mist above the sea is different: it contains many condensed droplets. Depth alone cannot tell us whether an explosion will happen, and seawater does not put out every volcano.

*Scene cue: Run shallow water to about 40%. Point separately to suspended underwater particles and white mist above the sea, then move to the endpoint to see deposited fragments.*

### A little island, a big mountain

Now we squeeze a long story into a short scene. Rounded lava lobes first overlap into a deep base. Broader flows then spread into low places. Near the surface, some fragments fall back onto the slope and later lava can cover them. Try Early stop: the seamount remains, but it never reaches the air. With continued supply, the visible island is still only a small part of the mountain. Waves reshape it at the end.

*Scene cue: Move through the island case and point to the four-stage strip and colored deposit bands. Near 75%, compare the two supplies and choose the section. Restore continued supply, move to 100%, and compare the dashed former outline.*
