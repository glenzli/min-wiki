Both devices move heat. Why can an air conditioner cool a room, while an open refrigerator cannot cool that same room continuously?

Follow the air-conditioning circuit, comparing cooling, fan-only and condensate. Switch to the refrigerator and compare air and food temperatures with a closed door and a midway opening. ## A sealed circuit: pressure, phase change and work

A typical split vapor-compression system has an indoor evaporator, outdoor compressor and condenser, and an expansion device. Fans promote heat exchange at coil surfaces. Tube walls separate air from refrigerant, so they do not mix in normal operation. Refrigerant pipes are not fresh-air ducts; ventilation needs a separate air path. The transparent casing is an observation view, not a way to operate equipment. Low-pressure refrigerant absorbs heat and evaporates indoors. Compressor work raises the vapor to a state that can reject heat and condense at a temperature above outdoor air. Throttling reduces pressure and flashes part of the liquid, returning a cooler mixture to the evaporator. Throttling is approximately isenthalpic; its temperature change depends on fluid properties. A steady cycle obeys Qout = Qin + W. The illustrated 3, 1 and 4 are consistent arbitrary teaching units, exclude fans and auxiliaries, and cannot establish a product’s efficiency.

## Condensate, comfort and temperature: heat loads and controls

Water vapor can condense when the coil surface is below the contacting air’s dew point. Moving air alone may improve comfort by increasing convective and evaporative heat loss from a person, but is not the same as removing room heat; fan electricity ultimately becomes heat. Actual room temperature depends on building heat capacity, outdoor heat transfer, sunlight, internal gains and system control. A refrigerant circuit time is not a room-cooling time. Wall conduction, door air exchange and warm food increase the load. Air and lining respond before the food interior, whose cooling also depends on mass, shape, conduction and convection. Two lumped heat capacities and separate start/stop thresholds illustrate delay here. A fixed-speed compressor stops after sufficient cooling and restarts as temperature rises; variable-speed designs can adjust capacity. The curves show trends, not when a particular food reaches safe storage conditions.

## Name the boundary before comparing balances

The shared reference retains cold-side heat, work input and hot-side heat. Air-conditioning uses arbitrary shares, the refrigerator uses cumulative kJ, and chapter three is an independent hypothetical cycle; these do not rank appliance efficiency. With both an open cold side and hot side in the same room, cyclic uptake and return cancel, leaving work as net input. Transient closed-door cooling also changes cabinet-air and food energy. Subtract room-to-cabinet leakage from condenser heat to obtain net room exchange. A fixed room temperature is not a prediction of actual room temperature.

## A common misconception

An air conditioner does not store cold air to pour out or make indoor heat disappear. Condensate comes from air’s water vapor, not normal refrigerant leakage. Fan-only operation is not active refrigeration.

## Model limits

Only a conceptual cooling-direction cycle is shown. Pipe layout, phase fractions, timing and energy shares are teaching choices. Pressure is relative, not measured data for a particular refrigerant. The air-conditioning model does not calculate room temperature, condensate quantity or thermostat control. Heat-pump heating needs reversing and control arrangements, not simply a backward-running compressor. The refrigerator separately models two heat capacities, door load and thermostat control on a compressed clock; it does not provide food-safety guidance. The room-boundary experiment does not simulate airflow or room temperature.

### Where heat and refrigerant travel

On a hot day, an air conditioner can slowly make a room cooler. Has the heat simply vanished? Let us look at the room and the outdoors together. There is a machine inside and another one outside, joined by pipes. With help from electricity, the system moves heat out of the room. The outdoor unit releases that heat, so its air can feel warm. Now make the casing transparent and find the copper-colored pipes. A material called refrigerant travels inside them to help move heat. It stays in sealed pipes instead of blowing onto our faces. Room air passes over a cooler coil and gives up some heat. A fan then sends that cooler air back into the room.

*Scene cue: Use the room-and-outdoors view; identify the indoor unit, wall and outdoor unit. Explore on screen only. Reveal the circuit and advance to 12%; distinguish refrigerant pipes from indoor air lines.*

### Follow refrigerant, then find the water

Our large gold dot marks one little parcel of refrigerant. It picks up heat indoors, then reaches the compressor. The compressor needs electricity. Next, the refrigerant can give up heat outdoors. After one more small gate, it returns to collect heat again. Look at the energy picture: the outdoors receives heat from the room, plus an extra share from the work used to run the cycle. Move closer to the indoor machine. Can you find water at the blue drain? The air already contains invisible water vapor. When it meets a cold enough coil, some can turn into little drops, like beads on the outside of a cold cup. Now try fan-only mode. Air can still move, but the heat-moving cycle is off. We can explore safely on screen and leave real equipment to adults and trained helpers.

*Scene cue: Play one cycle slowly, pausing at absorption, compression, rejection and expansion. Describe 3+1=4 as an example, not a measurement. Enlarge the indoor unit and compare humid and dry conditions. Switch to fan-only without changing progress; compare the circuit and energy bars.*

### Food cools slowly

There is food here, and air all around it. Start watching. The air cools first, but the middle of the food takes longer. Pause and find the two lines. Which changes faster? Now choose the scene with the opening door. Warmer room air comes in, giving the refrigerator more heat to carry away. After the door closes, cooling still takes time.

*Scene cue: Play and pause the closed-door scene, then compare both lines during the opening-door scene.*

### Put the hot side inside too

Draw a line around the whole room. Sending heat outdoors can leave less heat inside. But what if the hot side is also inside? The heat just removed comes back, and the work used by the machine stays too. A refrigerator doorway can feel cool briefly without cooling the whole room continuously. Both machines move heat. What matters is where the heat ends up.

*Scene cue: Choose Where heat stays; keep work fixed while moving the hot side between outdoors and the same room.*
