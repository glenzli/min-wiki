If a same-sized impactor hits the Moon twice as fast, does its kinetic energy only double?

Hold diameter fixed and compare two speeds, then hold speed fixed and change diameter. Inspect the energy readout, crater shape, and airborne ejecta.

## The impact brings kinetic energy

For a uniform spherical impactor, m=ρπD³/6 and E=mv²/2. At fixed density and diameter, doubling speed quadruples kinetic energy. At fixed density and speed, doubling diameter multiplies mass and energy by eight. Energy goes into shock compression, fragmentation, ejecta, melting, and heating. It does not all excavate the crater, and crater diameter does not scale directly with energy.

## Compression, excavation, and modification

High-speed contact launches shock waves. Moving material excavates a transient cavity and ejects debris, after which fallback and wall modification shape the final crater. Simple craters are broadly bowl-shaped; larger complex craters can have central peaks and terraces. Many ordinary oblique impacts still make roughly circular craters, though very shallow angles and target properties can change the form.

## A long but incomplete record

The Moon lacks a thick protective atmosphere and widespread wind and rain erosion, preserving many ancient impacts. Its surface is not unchanging: later impacts destroy or cover older craters, tiny impacts churn regolith, and ancient lava flooded some basins. Researchers combine overlap, crater density, remote sensing, and sample ages rather than dating a crater solely from its depth.

## A common misconception

Most lunar circular craters are impacts, not volcanoes, and do not require vertical arrival. Doubling energy does not double crater diameter.

## Model limits

The 3D terrain and ejecta are simplified illustrations, not full shock hydrodynamics or material failure. Crater scaling is qualitative. The flash indicates impact energy, not burning in a lunar atmosphere.

### Read the marks on the Moon

Many round pits cover the Moon. Like old footprints, they give us clues about what happened there. Many formed when rocks from space struck the ground. Watch one approach. The Moon does not have Earth’s thick atmosphere, so the rock does not experience the same atmospheric slowing and glowing meteor journey.

*Scene cue: Play the approach over the airless Moon.*

### After the collision

The fast rock strikes, compresses and breaks material, and sends fragments outward. Pause and look: where is the crater rim? Which pieces are still in the air? Then continue. Some pieces fall back, and the sides of the hole change. Its final shape is not completely finished at the instant of first contact.

*Scene cue: Pause four seconds during ejecta flight, then watch fallback.*

### Try a different speed

Keep the rock the same size and change only its speed. A faster impact brings much more energy. Then keep speed fixed and choose a larger rock. Changing one condition at a time helps us understand what affects the result, instead of simply choosing whichever collision looks more dramatic.

*Scene cue: Compare speeds, then diameters; point out the energy readout.*

### Why the marks last

On Earth, wind, rain, and rivers slowly change the ground. The Moon lacks this familiar weather, so many craters survive a long time. But new impacts can cover old marks, and tiny fragments stir its surface dust. The Moon is not a book whose pages never change. We need several clues to read its history.

*Scene cue: Finish with the 3D crater terrain; rotate the view to inspect its floor, raised rim, and ejecta blanket.*
