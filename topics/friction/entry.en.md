## Contact, relative sliding and energy

Kinetic friction generally opposes relative sliding at a contact. Here blocks move right over fixed horizontal surfaces, so friction acts left. Microscopic interactions and deformation matter, not just visible bumps catching. A coefficient describes a material pair and its condition, not one material name or appearance alone. After release on a horizontal surface with constant kinetic-friction coefficient, deceleration is μg, stopping time is v₀/(μg), and stopping distance is v₀²/(2μg). Doubling speed doubles stopping time and quadruples distance under those conditions. Once stopped, the block is not accelerated backward by a continuing friction force. Static friction adjusts within a limit and can help walking forward; it does not always oppose an object’s overall motion. Wheeled toys involve rolling, bearings and deformation, unlike these blocks. During sliding, mechanical energy becomes internal energy and other forms rather than disappearing. Warm hands after rubbing provide a qualitative clue, not a coefficient measurement. The bar uses the constant-friction model: remaining kinetic fraction is (v/v₀)² and the transferred fraction is its complement. Orange represents energy entering the block and surface, not a displayed temperature rise.

## From tire contact to the complete stopping distance

A vehicle travels while the driver reacts. With fixed reaction time, reaction distance is speed times that interval. Under constant deceleration, braking distance is speed squared divided by twice the deceleration. Doubling speed therefore doubles one part and quadruples the other; total distance follows neither factor exactly. Brakes slow wheel rotation while tire-road forces change vehicle motion. Kinetic energy is mainly converted to internal energy, with recovery possible in some vehicles. Grip, slope, tires, load and system condition affect stopping. The model wet-road setting is a comparison, not a universal multiplier for real roads. With no tire slip during ordinary braking, static friction transmits road force to the vehicle, while pad–disc sliding supplies braking torque and internal energy. The gold-point trajectory is ideal rigid-wheel kinematics; it does not solve tire deformation, ABS or locked-wheel braking from road settings.

## Passenger inertia and correctly fitted restraints

During a sudden stop the body needs a force to change its motion. Correct restraints manage deceleration and load stronger body regions. Children need seats appropriate for their size and installed according to manufacturer and local requirements. Boosters position the shoulder belt across shoulder and chest and the lap belt low over hips. Inertia is not an additional forward force; deceleration requires real external forces. Explaining a managed slowing interval does not justify loosening a belt. Never route the shoulder belt under an arm or behind the back. Children use suitable restraints in the rear seat according to product size and eligibility limits, not one birthday; US guidance is not a statement of local law. The side-view mechanism isolates an ideal lap band’s horizontal action. It runs from fixed anchors, touches the hip projection tangentially and wraps around its front. Forward travel lengthens the band and elastic tension pulls backward. The dashed reference lacks this horizontal restraint; inertia is still not a forward force. Belt force remains at the first zero relative speed, which does not mean the car has stopped relative to the ground. The undamped model would rebound afterward, so the observation window ends here.

## A common misconception

Forward motion does not imply sliding contact; static road friction differs from pad–disc sliding. Inertia is not an extra forward force, and belts must not be deliberately loosened.

## Model limits

The coefficients are illustrative, not measured values. The contact window depicts limited contact and relative sliding without solving microscopic deformation; orange and the energy bars do not measure temperature. Air drag is omitted and stopped blocks never reverse. The car separately uses a fixed one-second reaction and 6/3 m/s² decelerations; block coefficients are not applied to it. Vehicle energy fractions omit wheel rotation. Contact observation, block seconds and rescaled stopping time are separate clocks. No body forces, injuries or driving-distance advice are calculated. The belt-loading window is independent of the stopping clock. Mass, stiffness and displacement use teaching units; body travel and belt extension are exaggerated. It omits the shoulder belt, rotation, tissue deformation, dissipation, retractors and pretensioners, and does not predict real body loads or injury.

### Begin with the same start

Three identical blocks start equally fast. No one keeps pushing after release; contact with the surface slows them. Select a lane. Teal motion energy shrinks while orange transferred energy grows, keeping the same total. Would a faster start travel farther?

*Scene cue: Play together, select a contact, scrub back and replay, then change initial speed.*

### A rolling wheel and its contact

Now follow the same gold mark on a wheel. At the bottom, it touches the road without sliding along it for that instant. A locked wheel can instead slide as a whole. A car has two different contacts: pads rub the brake disc, and tires rely on the road’s force to slow the whole vehicle.

*Scene cue: Compare rolling and locked-wheel cases at the same displacement, then identify the disc and road contacts.*

### Reaction takes distance too

A driver needs time to react. In the gold section, this braking action has not started yet. In the green section the car slows. Increase starting speed, then change the road, and compare both sections. These numbers are teaching choices, never a guide for deciding when to cross a real road.

*Scene cue: Compare 30 versus 60 and dry versus wet separately; pause during reaction, braking and after stopping.*

### The body must slow too

Forces slow the car, and real forces must slow our bodies too. A correctly fitted belt and suitable child seat help. The shoulder belt crosses shoulder and chest; the lap belt lies low over the hips. Never loosen the belt deliberately or hide it behind the back. Adults choose and install protection using the child’s size and both manuals, and secure every trip. The band is snug from the start. As the body advances relative to the car, the band stretches and pulls backward. This enlarged illustration explains the principle; it is no reason to leave belt slack.

*Scene cue: Inspect shoulder and lap belts, then rear-facing, forward-facing and booster seats; show no unsafe-fit or injury experiment. Compare 0, 50 and 100% in the snug-band loading window. Call the endpoint the first maximum relative displacement, not a completed vehicle stop.*
