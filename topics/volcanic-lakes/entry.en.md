If a volcano leaves a large hollow, must it become a lake?

Choose a caldera and follow magma withdrawal, faulting and subsidence. Then keep the basin and compare plentiful supply with low leakage against limited supply with high leakage.

## Crater and caldera origins

Craters commonly involve explosion, buildup and local collapse near a vent. Calderas generally form when withdrawal from a magma reservoir allows extensive overlying rock to subside along faults. Size alone is not a complete classification, and familiar lake names do not always state their geological origin.

## A basin needs a water budget

Rain, snow, surface inflow and groundwater may supply a basin; evaporation, leakage and outflow remove water. Storage changes by inputs minus outputs over the same interval. Sustained net supply allows a lake to grow. As level rises, evaporation area, seepage and overflow can change, so basin depth alone does not determine the lake level.

## Water does not come from cooling lava

Magma mainly cools into rock, not a lake of ordinary water. Later water interacts with volcanic rocks, gases and hydrothermal fluids, producing varied temperature and chemistry. Some lakes are cool and clear; others are hazardous. Water level, temperature, chemistry and gas observations help distinguish their behavior.

## A common misconception

A quiet volcano does not automatically form a lake; water retention and sustained supply both matter.

## Model limits

Collapse and lake filling may span long periods compressed here. Real fault mechanics, lake chemistry and gas-release hazards are not simulated.

### First, a low place

Why can a lake sit high on a mountain? Some lakes occupy hollows left by volcanic activity. But a hollow cannot create water by itself. First we will see how this home forms, then ask where the water comes from. Choose the caldera story and look at the magma and rock above it.

*Scene cue: Choose the caldera and hold the underground section.*

### Rock settles downward

When some magma leaves its underground reservoir, the rock above may lose support and move down along fractures, leaving a large hollow. Advance slowly and notice the order. The whole mountain has not vanished, and the magma has not eaten every rock. The layers are still there, but their positions change.

*Scene cue: Advance withdrawal, faulting and collapse with two-second pauses.*

### Can water stay?

Rain, snow and other incoming water can now collect in the hollow. Increase supply and watch the level rise. Then allow more leakage. Predict whether the lake will end at the same depth. Pause and compare: both incoming and outgoing water determine how much remains in the basin.

*Scene cue: Compare supply and leakage settings, pausing for three seconds.*

### Beautiful lakes can differ

The water arrived later; red magma did not cool into blue water. Volcanic lakes can have very different temperatures and chemistry, and some have invisible gas hazards. A beautiful color is not an invitation to swim. Learn with the model and follow local rules with an adult when visiting real lakes.
