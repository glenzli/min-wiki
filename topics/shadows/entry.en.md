## Follow the light

The figure blocks some of the lamp's light. A higher lamp makes the centerline shadow shorter when horizontal separation stays fixed. The full figure still casts a footprint directly underneath an overhead lamp.

## Model and evidence

For a point source and a flat plane, $L=dh/(H-h)$, where $H$ is lamp height, $h$ is the height of a vertical occluder, $d$ is their horizontal separation, and $L$ is the ground projection of the occluder's centerline. This requires $H>h$ and does not describe the full outline of a figure with width. The controls use illustrative coordinates and grid divisions, not measured physical lengths. ## A shadow receives less illumination

In a uniform medium, geometric optics approximates light as straight rays. An opaque object blocks some rays, reducing illumination on a receiving surface. A shadow is not black material emitted by the object and need not be completely dark: other sources and reflections can illuminate it.

## Geometry for a point source

With puppet height h, lamp height H and horizontal separation d, similar triangles give centerline shadow length L = dh/(H−h), provided H exceeds h. Raising the lamp at fixed separation shortens the shadow; increasing separation at fixed height lengthens it. The controlled conditions are part of each conclusion. Each body part is also projected by ray-plane intersection, so the overhead footprint includes head, torso and arms. The formula refers only to the top-center point, not the maximum extent of the whole shadow.

## Real lamps and the Sun differ

Extended sources emit from many positions, producing full and partial shadow regions with softer edges. Sunlight over a small scene is approximately parallel because the Sun is very distant; tabletop point-source parameters cannot simply be reused. Outdoor shadow length changes with solar altitude. Observe shadows, never look into the Sun or strong lamps.

## A common misconception

A shadow is not always on one fixed side of an object; relative to this lamp, it extends away from the light.

## Model limits

Simplified 3D shapes are ray-projected onto flat ground. The top-center ray is not the maximum extent of the full shadow. Nine samples approximate an extended source; penumbra brightness and reflections are not calibrated.

### Where a shadow comes from

When the lamp shines, a darker patch appears beside the puppet. It is not a piece of black cloth dropped by the puppet. The puppet blocks some light from this lamp, so part of the ground receives less illumination. Find the lamp, puppet and shadow and notice their positions.

*Scene cue: Point to lamp, puppet and ground shadow in order.*

### Raise only the lamp

Keep the lamp from moving left or right and raise it. Predict whether the shadow will lengthen or shorten, then move the height slowly. Pause to compare the ground. Follow the drawn ray past the top of the puppet: the shadow boundary changes with the light’s direction.

*Scene cue: Hold horizontal position, raise lamp and pause for three seconds.*

### Change sides

Now keep the height steady and move the lamp slowly from the puppet’s left to its right. The shadow shortens, then stretches out on the opposite side. The puppet has not turned around. A shadow depends on where light comes from and where it lands, as well as the object itself. Pause and look from above. It is still the same shadow, seen from another viewpoint.

*Scene cue: Play the left-to-right sweep, pause overhead, switch to the overhead view, then scrub backward.*

### Try noticing everyday shadows

A real lamp is not a single point, so shadow edges can look soft. The Sun makes shadows too, but it is very far away and differs from a tabletop lamp. Ask an adult to investigate with a flashlight and block, and explain your discoveries. Watch the surface, without staring into a strong light.

*Scene cue: Pause with the lamp to one side; toggle the extended lamp and compare center and edge.*
