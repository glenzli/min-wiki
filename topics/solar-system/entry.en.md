If we descend from space, will every world eventually reveal ground to stand on?

Compare orbits and sizes, then select Earth, play cloud activity and choose a desert or ice-sheet site and scrub the descent. Try Jupiter’s storms and deeper atmosphere, find solar spots and prominences, then enter Titan from Saturn and return to its parent system.

## An orbit is a continuously turning path

A planet moves while the Sun’s gravity changes its velocity, bending its path. There is no physical rail underneath it. In the two-body approximation a bound orbit is an ellipse with the Sun at a focus. Equal areas are swept out in equal times, so a planet usually moves faster near perihelion and slower near aphelion.

## Appearance, terrain and interiors use different evidence

Whole-globe appearance is informed by spacecraft images, but a representative close view is not a surveyed landing site. Earth has rocky and liquid-water surfaces; Venus has rock hidden beneath clouds. The four giant planets have no Earth-like solid landing surface. Going deeper raises pressure and temperature. Cutaways combine seismic, gravity, rotation and physical-model constraints; thicknesses, colors and candidate boundaries are teaching devices, not photographs. The solar photosphere is not rocky ground either; fusion deep inside powers the Sun.

## Different animated views need different clocks

The orbit view preserves relative planetary periods. Moon views also use a shared clock and relative periods, but on a separate display timescale. Atmospheric flows and solar activity are continuous illustrations, not forecasts or magnetohydrodynamic calculations. Descent controls the observation camera and pauses independently of activity; its percentage is not a height, time or pressure measurement. Moon sizes and orbital spacing are scaled separately. Rings are particle populations; moons are individual orbiting bodies. Phases are a separate question about viewing and illumination geometry, with their own follow-up topic.

## A common misconception

Blue does not necessarily mean water, and bright clouds are not ground. The Sun is not burning rock, and moons are not another class of planet.

## Model limits

Terrain, weather and solar activity are simplified illustrations. Descent is not a survivable flight, free-fall calculation or live observation. The size comparison alone preserves diameter ratios; cutaway thicknesses and moon-system display scales are schematic.

### Meet the family

Let’s begin beside the Sun. The Sun makes its own light, and eight planets travel around it. Mercury, Venus, Earth, and Mars are closer in. Farther out are Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, and Neptune. You do not have to remember every name yet. Choose a world you like and find its path.

*Scene cue: Show the initial orbit overview.*

### What will we meet below?

Choose Earth and descend slowly from space. First comes the whole blue globe, then white clouds, and finally dunes or ice at the selected site. Pause and look: clouds move while land stays beneath them. Now try Jupiter. Will you find ground to stand on? Its bands and swirls are atmosphere, which becomes denser farther down.

*Scene cue: Pause Earth’s descent at the globe, clouds and close view. Compare the endpoint on Jupiter.*

### The Sun changes too

Return to the Sun. It shines with energy released by fusion deep inside. Look for small bright and dark granules and darker sunspots. Sunspots are cooler than their surroundings, but still extremely hot. Arches at the edge represent prominences, where magnetic fields support solar material. Pause to inspect them. There is no solid ground here.

*Scene cue: Play and pause solar activity. Identify granules, spots and prominences, then inspect the fusion core.*

### Companions around planets

Earth has the Moon, and Saturn has many moons too. They orbit their planets and travel with them around the Sun. Choose Titan and approach its hazy world. Its lakes contain mainly methane and ethane, not water. Return to Saturn afterward. The close world and the distant solar system belong to the same connected place.

*Scene cue: Compare Earth’s and Saturn’s moon systems, enter Titan, then use the parent button to return.*
