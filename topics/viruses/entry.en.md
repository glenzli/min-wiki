## A virus particle is not a complete cell

A virus particle is not a cell and cannot independently carry out a complete protein-making and reproduction process. This T4-like structural example has a protein head protecting DNA, a contractile sheath around a central tube, and fibers involved in surface binding. The fibers are not walking legs. Structural studies distinguish the shortening sheath from the tube, which does not shorten with it. Not every phage has a contractile tail; other viruses may have RNA or an envelope. The bacterium is a cell with DNA and ribosomes but no nucleus enclosing its DNA.

## Attachment and replication are separate barriers

A phage must interact with suitable receptors for this entry pathway. Successful binding does not guarantee replication: bacterial defences and physiological state can block subsequent steps. Surface mismatch and internal defence therefore represent distinct barriers; a key-and-lock analogy alone is an incomplete account of host range.

## Replication, assembly and release differ

In a cell supporting this lytic pathway, viral DNA replication involves the required enzymes, while protein synthesis depends on host ribosomes, materials and energy. This does not mean the virus manufactures itself independently. T4 assembly research shows DNA packaging through a head portal, followed by neck and tail attachment: making components and assembling a particle are distinct events. The scene preserves this sequence and opens the host envelope before offspring pass through. Actual lysis involves disruption of membranes and peptidoglycan, not phages kicking the cell open. Other viruses may bud from cells, and some phages can enter lysogeny.

## A common misconception

Being able to bind a cell does not guarantee replication, and viruses do not reproduce simply by growing and splitting like bacteria.

## Model limits

This is one tailed-phage lytic route with T4-like structural references, not a universal model of viruses or human infection. The transparent cutaway, colors, envelope spacing and six representative offspring are teaching choices. DNA is a single drawn strand with exaggerated coils. Molecular motion, packaging motors, membrane channels, host DNA degradation and diffusion are not simulated. The offspring are not immature little viruses that must grow. Scrubbing backward is a viewing aid, not reversible infection.

### Meet an unusual tiny visitor

The little object that looks like a probe is a bacteriophage, a virus that infects certain bacteria. Choose Attachment close-up. The golden strand in its head represents genetic information. The fibers below help it bind a surface; they are not walking legs. It is neither a tiny robot nor a complete cell, and it cannot make new viruses by itself.

*Scene cue: Before encounter, choose Attachment close-up; observe the head, DNA and fibers without advancing progress.*

### The first barrier

Bring it towards the bacterium. Suitable surface structures are needed for attachment. Try a mismatch and pause to see where progress stops. With a suitable example, genetic information can enter, yet replication is still not guaranteed: defences inside some cells can block later steps.

*Scene cue: Pause at mismatch, then compare internal defence.*

### Make parts and assemble them

Now choose the cell that supports replication and select Watch the process. Look inside the cell: it already has machinery for making proteins. With the cell’s help, new components form, genetic information goes into heads, and tails attach. Move the slider slowly and pause. Can you find a head that is not finished yet? The original virus is not growing bigger and splitting in two.

*Scene cue: In Inside the cell, scrub between production and assembly; pause for three seconds to inspect portal packaging and tail attachment.*

### One pathway among many

In this example the bacterium lyses and releases new phages. The few drawings stand for a process, not a measured number. Other viruses leave cells in other ways. Remember the key distinction: meeting a cell, entering it and successfully replicating are separate events.

*Scene cue: Move offspring continuously outward and end with host comparisons.*
