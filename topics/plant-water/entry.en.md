How can water reach a leaf above the roots without a mechanical pump?

Play the complete root-to-air journey and pause or scrub backward. Filled blue markers indicate liquid water; open circles indicate vapour. In the leaf detail, first find evaporation from wet mesophyll surfaces into internal air spaces, then trace vapour leaving through the lower pore selected for this example.

## Water potential drives uptake

Root surfaces, including root hairs on young regions, provide uptake interfaces. Water moves through walls and cells and encounters selective root barriers before entering transport tissue. Direction depends on water-potential differences. Even moist soil can supply water poorly when salinity is high or roots are damaged.

## Transpiration is linked to a continuous water column

Evaporation from internal leaf surfaces and vapour diffusion through stomata lower leaf water potential and create tension in xylem. Cohesion between water molecules and interaction with conduit walls help maintain a connected column. Root pressure and capillarity can contribute, but capillarity alone does not explain sustained transport in tall plants.

## Stomata mediate a water–carbon trade-off

Guard cells regulate pore opening. Open pores allow carbon dioxide entry but also water loss. Light, wind, humidity, temperature and soil supply influence transpiration. A hot day does not invariably mean wider pores: drought may trigger closure. The blue route represents water, not the transport of sugars.

## A common misconception

Xylem flow is not driven by a mechanical pump in the root; transpiration and water-potential gradients are central to the explanation.

## Model limits

Twenty-two seconds is an observation duration. Blue markers trace selected cohorts, not actual water quantities. Whole-plant and leaf-detail views use different scales. Vapour is normally invisible; water potential, cavitation and dynamic pore regulation are not solved.

### Travel with water

After watering a plant, where does the water go? Begin in the soil and follow a little of it. Roots have no mouth, but water can pass through their surfaces into the plant. Our blue dot is a tracking symbol, not a giant blue ball inside a real root.

*Scene cue: Begin at the root and introduce the tracking symbol.*

### Enter the narrow routes

At the next stop, water moves upward through xylem, the plant’s water-carrying tissue. Pause and trace the connection to a leaf. There is no hidden electric pump. Water leaving the leaf helps pull on connected water below, linking roots, stem and leaves in the journey.

*Scene cue: Move continuously through stem and vein and pause.*

### The leaf’s tiny openings

Water helps support cells and participates in making sugars. Much of it evaporates inside leaves and leaves through tiny stomatal pores. Cells around each pore adjust its opening. Open pores let carbon dioxide in, but also let water vapour out, so the plant must balance both needs. Play the connected route, then open the leaf close-up. Inside the leaf, the blue marker becomes an open vapour symbol before passing through the pore into the air. Pause the journey to inspect that small passage.

*Scene cue: Switch to the leaf detail and pause as liquid enters leaf tissue, vapour reaches the pore, and vapour leaves.*

### An invisible departure

Water vapour is normally invisible; leaves are not spraying large blue drops. The amount lost varies with plant and weather. Trace the whole route once more, from root to air. You have followed one small part of how plants return soil water to the atmosphere in the water cycle.

*Scene cue: Review all four stops and fade the marker into the air.*
