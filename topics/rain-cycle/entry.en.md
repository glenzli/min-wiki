## Energy and changes of state

Solar radiation supplies much of the energy for evaporation, which also occurs below boiling. Cooling can allow vapor to condense on tiny particles. Visible clouds contain droplets, ice crystals or both; gaseous water is invisible. Evaporation absorbs latent heat and condensation releases it, linking water movement to atmospheric energy.

## Gravity connects water stores

The same rain can partition differently between infiltration, surface runoff and temporary storage on different surfaces. This page compares fixed teaching fractions; real outcomes also depend on rainfall intensity, slope, previous moisture, structure and cracks. Water follows the visible hillside rill into the same valley, lake and outlet; bed, water and tracer routes share one geometry. Infiltrated water may later pass through unsaturated soil to recharge an aquifer, so infiltration is not already recharge. Groundwater travels through connected pores and fractures rather than a universal cavern river. Hydraulic-head differences can make it feed a river or receive river water. The separate long-term experiment balances recharge, exchange, pumping and storage in simplified boxes; it does not calculate real 3D flow or water purification.

## Glaciers store water on slower timescales

Mountain glaciers require snow to survive repeated summers and gradually become firn and ice. Compaction reduces volume without deleting water, so the longer-term experiment uses water equivalent for its budget. Accumulation minus loss gives the net balance. Sustained losses can make the terminus retreat while ice still moves downstream. Above, a batch travels through an existing glacier; below, a separate model balances snow, firn and ice over teaching years. Those quantities must not be combined. Terminus geometry and years are uncalibrated, not a full stress, deformation or climate prediction.

## A common misconception

A cloud is not a bag of invisible vapor: its visible parts are tiny droplets or ice crystals.

## Model limits

The 100 portions are not global water proportions, measured rainfall or a complete catchment water balance. The main scene counts current teaching locations; terrain, clouds, tracers, pores and time use different illustrative scales, and batch counts do not drive river or lake levels. Ice melting into rain in a warm layer below a cloud is distinct from high-mountain snowfall. The existing glacier’s slow batch clock and the longer-term groundwater and glacier studies have separate budgets and time scales. They cannot be combined or used for local water-level, glacier or climate forecasts. Full 3D aquifer flow, glacier stress and deformation, contaminant purification and plant transpiration are not simulated.

## Narration {#narration}

### Start at the sea

Have you seen wet ground slowly dry after rain? The ground has not eaten the water. Some has become invisible water vapor and entered the air. This happens over the sea too. Sunshine supplies energy for the journey. Our gold circle is a little pointer helping us follow one portion of water.

*Scene cue: Point to the sea and pause for two seconds.*

### Into a cloud

Under suitable conditions, water vapor condenses into droplets and the cloud becomes visible. Enlarging the same cloud shows droplets meeting and merging. Some clouds also contain ice. A cloud does not guarantee rain at the ground: very dry air below can turn the falling rain back into vapor.

*Scene cue: Advance into the cloud and pause for three seconds.*

### Rain falls

The tiny particles in clouds must grow before they can fall as rain or snow. This time we follow rain onto a hillside. Earth pulls water downward, and streams and rivers carry some back to the sea. Watch the circle travel along the winding river: the water does not simply jump back into the ocean.

*Scene cue: Advance slowly through rainfall and along the river.*

### Water that stays longer

Different portions take different routes. Some enter little spaces in soil and rock and slowly feed a river. Some fall as snow high in the mountains. Over a longer time, snow that stays can gradually become ice. Ice moves slowly downhill too. If more water is lost than arrives, a glacier gets smaller, and underground water stores can shrink too. Water has no compulsory final stop. Some changes are worth watching slowly.

*Scene cue: First compare batch ice and meltwater, then enter the two independent longer-term studies: groundwater recharge versus withdrawal and glacier accumulation versus loss. Keep separate clocks and quantities visible.*
