When Beijing’s days become longer, do days in the Southern Hemisphere also become longer?

In the seasons scene, compare June and December for Beijing and a southern city. Then try the untilted-Earth scene and compare daylight changes.

## Tilt changes illumination geometry

Earth’s axis is about 23.4° from the normal to its orbital plane and keeps an approximately fixed direction over a year. As Earth orbits, solar declination changes between the tropics, changing noon solar altitude and daylight duration. More direct sunlight distributes a beam over a smaller horizontal area; longer days increase the time available to receive solar energy.

## Calculating geometric daylight

For a spherical Earth, a point Sun, and no atmospheric refraction, the sunrise hour angle obeys cos H₀=−tanφ tanδ. Daylight is approximately 24H₀/π hours, with latitude φ and declination δ. Values outside the inverse-cosine domain require polar-day or polar-night cases. Actual sunrise also depends on the solar disk, refraction, elevation, and terrain.

## Seasons are not a weather forecast

Opposite hemispheres lean toward and away from the Sun at the same time, explaining their generally opposite seasons. Earth’s changing Sun distance is real, but is not the main cause of its present seasons. Ocean heat storage, clouds, ice reflectivity, and atmospheric transport affect temperature, so the hottest day need not coincide with the summer solstice.

## A common misconception

Earth does not tilt back and forth to create each season. Its axis stays approximately fixed while its position around the Sun changes.

## Model limits

The readouts describe illumination geometry, not weather. Terrain, refraction, and ocean heat storage are omitted; displayed bodies and distances do not share a true scale.

### Find our home

Imagine standing on Earth while it spins and carries us around the Sun. The invisible line it spins around is tilted. It does not lean left today and right tomorrow. During this year-long journey, it keeps pointing roughly the same way. Start by finding that axis in the picture.

*Scene cue: Show the axis in the seasons overview.*

### Spread out the sunlight

At different places along Earth’s journey, sunlight reaches us at different angles. Sometimes it arrives more directly and is more concentrated. Sometimes it arrives at a slant and the same beam spreads out farther. The amount of time we have daylight changes too. Angle and daylight time work together to shape our seasons.

*Scene cue: Compare solar altitude and illuminated area without swinging the axis.*

### Compare both sides

Pause and make a guess. When Beijing’s days get longer, what happens in a city south of the equator? Take a moment, then switch cities and inspect the numbers. Both places do not always gain daylight together. As one hemisphere leans more toward the Sun, the other leans away, giving them generally opposite seasons.

*Scene cue: Compare northern and southern cities in June and December; pause four seconds.*

### Try a different Earth

Finally, try an Earth whose axis is not tilted. Does daylight in the same city change through the year as much as before? This is a small experiment inside our picture. In the real world, clouds, oceans, and winds affect warmth too. Understanding seasons does not mean a sunlight diagram can predict tomorrow’s weather.

*Scene cue: Switch to the untilted scene and compare daylight in the same city.*
