## State what a classification describes

Astronomical classes are not simply boxes ordered by size. Rocky and gaseous describe composition; star, planet and moon concern physical and orbital roles; main sequence and white dwarf concern evolution. Learning families organise this guide, while a separate noun-level label distinguishes bodies, material, systems, events and regions.

## Appearance is one line of evidence

Emission, reflection, wavelength, distance, obscuration and viewing direction all influence appearance. Planets reflect visible sunlight and emit thermal radiation. Nebulae can emit, scatter or block light. Spiral arms can become hard to see edge-on without the galaxy changing type. Classification usually combines observations and models, rather than relying on a silhouette.

## Separate membership from history

The Solar System belongs to the Milky Way, which belongs to the Local Group: these are membership relationships. Star formation, evolution and some stellar explosions are processes with different possible outcomes. A supernova event, its material remnant and a possible compact central object are connected but are not synonymous.

## A common misconception

Familiar astronomical names refer to different kinds of things: a meteor is atmospheric light, a nebula is material, a galaxy is a system and a supernova is an event.

## Model limits

These recognition illustrations compress size differences while preserving the known size order of representative bodies, without a true physical scale. Clouds, systems, events and black holes of unspecified mass use separate close-up windows rather than invented common diameters. The views are teaching illustrations, not photographs or an observational classifier. Stellar energy-cutaway colours and arrows explain the energy source and outward transfer, not measured layer thicknesses. Point samples are not actual object counts. Common examples are selected rather than every transitional class. Each linked study owns its scientific model, scale and uncertainties. The paths are learning sequences, not universal evolutionary sequences. Stellar glare uses compressed brightness; granules and starspots are enlarged representative textures, and the white dwarf is a hot blue-white example. Nebula shapes, gas, dust lanes and colours are constructed teaching illustrations, not a 3D reconstruction of an observed nebula or a measurement of density, temperature or spectra.

### What kind of thing is it?

The sky has points of light and sometimes a streak that quickly disappears. These belong to astronomy, but they are not all the same. Choose a name. Is it one body, some material, a group of members or something happening?

*Scene cue: Compare the Sun with a meteor.*

### Where does the light come from?

The Sun obtains energy from within. The Moon reflects sunlight. Some clouds glow, while others block light from behind them. Both bright and dark appearances invite a question: where does the light come from? One clue alone cannot identify everything.

*Scene cue: Compare the Sun, Moon and an interstellar cloud.*

### Some names describe a group

The Solar System has the Sun, planets and many smaller members. The Milky Way has many stellar systems. A star cluster groups stars, while a galaxy group brings galaxies together. Read the name carefully to discover which level you are exploring.

*Scene cue: Switch between a star cluster, galaxy and galaxy group.*

### Take a question with you

Choose a path. Follow a comet toward the Sun, compare the endings of stars, explore the effects of gravity or rotate a galaxy. Take one question into each study, and return with a new question to explore.

*Scene cue: Follow a learning path into an experiment.*
