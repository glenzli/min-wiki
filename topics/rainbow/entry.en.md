## From sky to drop

A rainbow surrounds the antisolar direction. It describes directions around the observer, not a coloured wall at a fixed distance. A higher Sun places the antisolar point lower. Flat, opaque ground blocks the lower view; above a solar altitude of roughly the primary bow's 42° radius, this ground view can place the entire primary bow below the horizon.

The unobstructed full-circle view removes horizon occlusion and assumes suitable sunlit drops in all relevant directions. Being high up or aboard an aircraft does not guarantee a complete bow.

## Refraction, reflection and colour

At a water–air boundary, directions obey $n_1\sin i=n_2\sin r$. The angles $i$ and $r$ are measured from the surface normal, and $n_1$, $n_2$ are the media's refractive indices.

The primary branch in a spherical drop has two refractions and one partial internal reflection. Its angular radius from the antisolar direction is $\theta_1=4r-2i$. The secondary branch has one more internal reflection and $\theta_2=180^\circ+2i-6r$. These formulas describe different branches and are not interchangeable.

Near the primary extremum, red lies at about 42° and violet at a smaller radius, placing red outside violet. The secondary ordering is reversed. Readings use the existing topic's representative refractive indices and spherical-drop geometry. The natural spectrum is continuous; seven labels select representative colours.

Solid paths trace the main rainbow branch. Translucent dashed paths help distinguish complementary reflected or transmitted directions. Brightness does not encode energy fractions, and this is not a total-internal-reflection demonstration.

## Colors leave in different directions

White light contains a continuous range of visible wavelengths. Refraction changes its direction across air–water boundaries, with a slightly different refractive index for each wavelength. A primary-bow path includes refraction, one internal reflection, and refraction on exit. Nearby rays concentrate around particular exit angles. The droplet does not manufacture colors, and internal reflection is not necessarily total reflection.

## The observer determines the arc

A primary bow is centered on the antisolar point, with visible red near the outer 42° edge and violet slightly inward. Different colors reaching one observer usually arrive from droplets in different directions. There is no fixed endpoint on the ground. Raising the Sun lowers the antisolar point and usually reduces the arc visible above the horizon. An unobstructed elevated view can sometimes reveal more of the circle.

## The secondary bow and model limits

Two internal reflections produce the fainter secondary bow outside the primary, with reversed colors. A darker Alexander’s band often lies between them. Droplet sizes, shapes, and wave effects can produce additional fine bands. The separated rays and representative wavelengths here explain geometry, rather than calculating the brightness of every droplet in a real sky.

## A common misconception

A rainbow is not a bridge fixed in one place. Sunlight, droplets, and the observer determine it; the secondary is not a mirror image suspended in the sky.

The ray diagram approximates spherical droplets and selected wavelengths, without full interference, polarization, drop distributions, or exposure. Screen band widths are not calibrated measurements.

### Colors inside sunlight

Sometimes a rainbow appears after rain. Let’s enlarge one tiny water drop. Light that looks white contains many colors. It bends on entering the drop, some reflects inside, and it bends again when leaving. Different colors bend by slightly different amounts, so they leave in different directions.

### Which light reaches your eyes?

One drop does not line up every color and send them all into your eyes. Many drops fill the sky. Different colors arrive from different directions and together make a curved band. Find the Sun and the eye in the picture. When you look at a rainbow, the Sun is usually behind you.

*Scene cue: Show Sun, observer, and antisolar direction in sky view.*

### Guess, then move

Pause for a moment. If we raise the Sun, will the rainbow rise too? Make your guess and slowly move the solar-height control. Notice how the part above the horizon moves lower instead. The ground can hide the lower part of the circle. A different view can let us see more of its curve.

*Scene cue: Pause for four seconds, raise solar altitude, and compare ground and unobstructed views.*

### Look for another bow

If there is a second, fainter rainbow, look for its red edge. Its colors run the other way around because the light reflected one extra time inside the drops. There is no rainbow foot you can run to and no bridge you can stand on. Sunlight, water drops, and your eyes make this beautiful sight together.

*Scene cue: Compare red in the primary and secondary bows.*
