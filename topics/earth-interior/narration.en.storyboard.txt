01 · Find the thin outer shell
[Compare the common-scale cutaway with the separate shallow enlargement; do not compare their apparent thicknesses directly.]

The rock under our feet feels hard, but Earth is not one solid stone ball. Its outer crust is thin. Beneath it are a thick mantle and a metallic core. Compare their thicknesses with one shared ruler first.

---

02 · Solid material can slowly deform
[Identify crust, uppermost mantle and lithosphere together in the shallow enlargement.]

Most of the mantle is solid. During an earthquake wave it responds as a solid, yet over an enormous span of time it can slowly deform. A tectonic plate includes crust and some uppermost mantle, rather than crust alone.

---

03 · Waves bring clues from the interior
[Switch P and S waves and receivers. Qualify shadows as principal direct-wave results, and compare mantle xenoliths with iron meteorites.]

Earthquakes send out different waves. Some cross liquids; others cannot cross in their original shearing form. Records from many locations help scientists investigate the hidden interior. Rock samples provide another clue, but cannot answer every question.

---

04 · Zoom into the same rift
[Hold the stage at Rift A while changing whole, regional and P1-material scales. Pause geology before replaying F1 and retain its offset. Compare unloaded, no-melt, early-cessation and underground-intrusion cases. Twenty tags split into arbitrary portions rather than a measured melt fraction.]

A continent can slowly stretch while rocks move across faults. Follow the same place downward. One branch shows rock storing deformation before rapid slip. Another follows mostly solid mantle material: only part melts under suitable conditions, and it can cool underground. The branches are related, but an earthquake does not shake the rocks into magma.
