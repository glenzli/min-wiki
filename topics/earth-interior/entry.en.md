## what lies beneath us, and how do we know? {#interior}

### Is Earth's interior really a layered cake?

The circles in a cutaway identify regions; the real planet has no neat colored fillings. Composition distinguishes crust, mantle and core. Crust and mantle consist mainly of rocky material with different minerals and compositions. The core is predominantly iron, with nickel and other elements. Physical state also matters: the outer core is liquid, whereas the inner core is solid.

The table gives this model's reference scales. Depth is measured inward from the surface; radius is measured outward from the center. Do not mix them. Rounded depths of about 2890 km for the core-mantle boundary and 5150 km for the inner-core boundary are calculated by subtracting the adopted boundary radii from the 6371 km reference radius. [NASA Earth parameters](https://nssdc.gsfc.nasa.gov/planetary/factsheet/earthfact.html), [NASA structural reference values](https://science.nasa.gov/earth/facts/)

| Region | Location or scale adopted here | Physical state to remember |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Crust | Representative oceanic crust: 5 km; continental crust: 30 km | Solid rock; actual thickness varies by region |
| Mantle | Below the crust to about 2890 km depth | Mostly solid, capable of slow deformation over long periods |
| Outer core | About 2890-5150 km depth | Predominantly liquid metal |
| Inner core | About 5150 km depth to the center; reference radius 1221 km | Predominantly solid metal |

High temperature does not automatically mean melting: pressure and composition matter too. Mantle material behaves as a solid during the short passage of seismic waves but can creep over geological time. Some regions contain small amounts of partial melt. That does not make the whole mantle an ocean of magma. [USGS: are plates floating on magma?](https://www.usgs.gov/faqs/are-tectonic-plates-floating-magma)

### Why is the crust thin but the lithosphere thicker?

Crust mainly describes composition; lithosphere describes mechanical behavior. **The lithosphere includes the crust and the relatively cool, rigid uppermost mantle.** Tectonic plates are large parts of this layer. One plate can carry both continental and oceanic regions. The weaker asthenosphere beneath is still mostly solid, rather than a liquid provided for plates to float on.

The whole-Earth cutaway uses one radius scale, making the crust nearly a line. A separate shallow-depth enlargement reveals the crust and lithosphere. It has its own ruler. A roughly 100 km lithosphere is a representative illustration, not a worldwide fixed boundary: thickness varies with region, temperature and plate age. [USGS: Earth's interior and lithosphere](https://pubs.usgs.gov/gip/dynamic/inside.html)

Imagine a globe with a radius of 1 m. At that scale, the model's 30 km continental crust is only about 4.7 mm thick; 5 km oceanic crust is about 0.8 mm thick. These are calculations with one length scale, not measurements of a physical crust sample. The whole globe explains relative size; a labeled enlargement makes thin structures readable.

### Are continents granite and ocean floors basalt?

These are useful representatives, rather than two uniform materials. Typical oceanic crust is thinner, with basalt and its coarse-grained deeper counterpart, gabbro, often beneath sediment. Continental crust is usually thicker and contains many igneous, metamorphic and sedimentary rocks. Granite is an important member, not an entire continent. Submerged continental crust also exists: being under the sea does not automatically make crust oceanic. [USGS interior structure](https://pubs.usgs.gov/gip/dynamic/inside.html), [NPS igneous compositions](https://www.nps.gov/subjects/geology/igneous.htm)

### How do we know there is liquid inside without drilling there?

Earthquakes generate several kinds of waves. P waves compress and stretch material along their direction of travel and can cross solids and liquids. S waves shear material sideways and cannot pass through the liquid outer core as ordinary shear waves. Waves can also reflect, refract or convert at boundaries. Worldwide recordings reveal which waves arrived, when they arrived, and where particular direct principal waves are missing.

In a basic Earth model, direct S waves do not cross the outer core to receivers beyond roughly 103° from the source. P waves have a principal shadow zone at about 103-140°. These are angles at Earth's center between the source and receiver, not longitude differences. A shadow concerns a particular direct wave; converted, reflected and other weaker signals may still appear in real recordings. It does not mean the ground there never shakes. [IRIS / EarthScope seismic shadows](https://www.iris.edu/hq/inclass/animation/seismic_shadow_zone_basic_introduction)

### Can a rock specimen replace seismic evidence?

They answer different questions. Outcrops and drill cores provide direct material. Some volcanic rocks carry xenoliths: fragments brought upward from deeper regions. Mineral assemblages and experiments help infer the fragments' original pressure, temperature and depth. One study of a mantle-peridotite xenolith in kimberlite interpreted its source as roughly 130-180 km deep. Such estimates depend on the sample and assumptions; they do not provide a sample of the whole mantle. [McCallum and Eggler, 1976](https://www.usgs.gov/publications/diamonds-upper-mantle-peridotite-nodule-kimberlite-southern-wyoming)

Iron meteorites offer another clue. Many are pieces of metallic cores from asteroids that melted and differentiated, showing how metal can gather inside a body. They are samples of other bodies, **not fragments taken from Earth's core**. Understanding Earth's core requires seismic evidence, bulk density, experiments and planetary materials together. [NASA JPL: origins of iron meteorites](https://www.jpl.nasa.gov/news/curiosity-mars-rover-checks-odd-looking-iron-meteorite/)

### Do separating, colliding and sliding plates produce the same landscapes?

Their relative motion matters. These are representative cases; real boundaries can have oblique motion and complicated histories.

| Condition | Possible results | Why compare it with another case? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Divergence | Rifts or mid-ocean ridges; decompression melting can supply new crust in some regions | Separation does not melt the entire mantle |
| Subduction of older, colder oceanic lithosphere | Trenches, earthquake zones and, in suitable locations, volcanic arcs | Lithosphere sinks; seawater is not simply pressing it down |
| Continental collision | Crustal shortening, thickening and mountain building | Every collision is not the same subduction-volcano sequence |
| Transform motion | Sideways displacement and frequent earthquakes | It need not continually create or destroy crust |
| Little relative motion | Less boundary deformation in this illustration | Changed conditions should allow weaker or absent results |

Internal heat and gravity participate in the plate system; the pull of cold, dense sinking slabs is among the important forces. Manual progress compares geometrical changes without assigning years or forecasting a particular earthquake or eruption. [USGS: understanding plate motions](https://pubs.usgs.gov/gip/dynamic/understanding.html)

### From the whole planet into the same rift

The fourth station connects three scales rather than replacing the location. Rift A names a weakness in an idealized continent. The regional view keeps that name: F1 is an inward-dipping normal fault on the basin’s western side, and P1 starts as mantle material below. Scale changes retain geological stage and identities. Coasts, terrain and grain domains are authored explanatory geometry, without a modern surface map passed off as paleogeography or a future forecast.

| Observation scale | What stays the same | New question and comparison |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Whole continent | Rift A and its extension stage | Continuing extension can proceed toward breakup; early cessation retains a basin without inevitable ocean formation |
| Regional section | F1 contacts, layered markers and P1 source | One branch follows locking, strain and rapid slip; another follows thinning, upwelling and melt transport |
| P1 material | The same twenty teaching tags | Distinguish solid residue, melt and newly crystallized portions |

A continental rift is not an empty crack to Earth’s center. Extension can lower a basin along normal faults in brittle upper rock while hotter material deforms slowly below. Real rifts segment, reuse inherited structures, migrate or cease activity. No continuing extension changes the premise from this case’s start; it is neither a pause button nor proof that real Earth has no stored stress. The late continuing case separately specifies connection to the sea, displayed in both whole and regional views, without solving sea level or water entry. [USGS plate motions](https://pubs.usgs.gov/gip/dynamic/understanding.html), [Brune and colleagues’ 2023 review](https://www.nature.com/articles/s43017-023-00391-3)

### F1: why replay one earthquake separately from geology?

Long-term extension can be shared among locking, creep and many earthquakes. F1 selects a mechanism replay with **stored elastic strain**, rather than forecasting a real earthquake: nearby rock deforms, then the hanging wall moves down-dip and into the basin. Offset remains after waves pass. The selected event’s displacement fades into the basin instead of silently slipping the opposite fault too. Correlatable layered markers terminate on their own final inclined contacts, making actual offset checkable. [USGS elastic rebound](https://www.usgs.gov/media/videos/elastic-rebound), [EarthScope/IRIS normal-fault evidence in rifts](https://web-archive-2022.iris.edu/hq/science_highlights/damaging_quakes_in_the_east_african_rift_zone_moderate_quakes_cause_great_damage_in_small_african_villages_mozambique_experiences_damaging_rift_related_earthquakes)

An unloaded control does not generate unexplained slip. Starting F1 pauses and preserves the geological stage; returning to the whole view never resumes either replay automatically. Roughly three seconds for the event presentation and eighteen for geology are interface choices, not real event durations or a fixed conversion to geological years. The strain bar has no stress units, and expanding lines do not calculate magnitude, seismograms or hazard.

### P1: why might only part of the rising material melt?

The other branch follows P1 from the same regional section. As predominantly solid mantle rises, decreasing pressure can move its path across the solidus and generate partial basaltic melt. Temperature, composition and water affect the result; simply being hot does not guarantee melting. The two thermal settings mean crossing or not crossing this case’s conditions, without a real pressure–temperature scale. Changing F1 event progress never changes P1 melting, ascent or crystallization. There is no shaking-rock-into-magma causal chain here. [McKenzie and Bickle’s original 1988 research](https://academic.oup.com/petrology/article-abstract/29/3/625/1432023)

P1 melt appears at its source before a transport front rises. A permitted pathway allows shallower intrusion; restricted transport retains material deeper to cool. Neither automatically reaches a surface eruption. Seismic evidence and modelling at Tanganyika–Rukwa have been interpreted as underground melt lenses, illustrating magmatism without surface volcanism. This is not an observed section of that region; a single velocity or color cannot independently establish magma. [Ajala and colleagues, 2024](https://www.nature.com/articles/s43247-024-01244-7)

P1 keeps twenty tags, each able to split into residual, molten and crystallized portions. The three readouts are arbitrary teaching portions totaling twenty before rounding. Selecting four melt-capable tags aids tracking; **four out of twenty is not a measured melt fraction**. A continuous residual domain remains at the source while the corresponding molten portion migrates. Slower and faster cooling compare larger grains with finer grains and dark groundmass. This interprets basaltic granular textures, without identifying particular minerals, growing quartz clusters or displaying atomic lattices. Real crystallization includes nucleation, growth and chemical reaction. [USGS cooling and igneous textures](https://www.usgs.gov/media/images/components-igneous-rocks)

## Two ways to define layers

Crust, mantle and core mainly distinguish composition. The mechanically defined lithosphere includes the crust and relatively rigid uppermost mantle. Most mantle material is solid but can deform slowly over geological time. The outer core is liquid and the inner core solid. Temperature alone does not determine melting: pressure and composition matter too.

## One radius scale and a separate enlargement

The model adopts a 6371 km Earth radius, 3485 km core radius and 1221 km inner-core radius, giving rounded boundary depths of about 2890 and 5150 km. Oceanic crust at 5 km and continental crust at 30 km are representatives. A separate shallow-depth ruler reveals thin layers; roughly 100 km of lithosphere is not a universal thickness.

## Several lines of evidence constrain the deep interior

P waves can cross solids and liquids; ordinary S shear waves cannot cross the liquid outer core. A basic model lacks direct core-crossing S waves beyond roughly 103 degrees and has a principal P shadow at about 103-140 degrees. This does not exclude weak or converted phases. Mantle xenoliths are real material whose source depths are inferred. Iron meteorites usually sample metallic parts of other differentiated bodies, not Earth's core. In a rift, deformation, fault records, seismic velocities and anisotropy, heat flow and rock chemistry provide different constraints. F1’s preloaded event and P1’s decompression melting are two branches at one site, without one driving the other. No surface eruption does not automatically exclude underground melt.

## A common misconception

Crust is not the whole lithosphere, and plates are not thin sheets floating on a magma ocean. Continents contain many rock types, not just granite. A seismic shadow does not mean no ground motion. Iron meteorites are not direct samples of Earth's core. Extension does not require an earthquake at every increment, and an earthquake does not shake rock into magma. No volcano does not prove no subsurface magmatism; not every rift becomes an ocean.

## Model limits

Whole-Earth circles share one radius scale; the shallow enlargement has a separate depth ruler. Colors are not interior photographs. Representative crustal and lithospheric thicknesses do not describe every site. Seismic paths explain basic principal waves and shadows, without calculating real travel times, speeds or complete phases. Plate deformation is illustrative; manual progress is neither years nor a hazard forecast. Rift A, F1 and P1 are authored idealized explanatory objects, not a modern map, paleogeographic reconstruction or forecast. Shapes are separately enlarged across scales, without a shared length ruler. Geological and local-event replays are independent and mutually exclusive; their presentation durations are not real process durations. Twenty P1 tags may split into phases whose arbitrary portions total twenty within this ledger, without representing a measured melt fraction or mineral identification.

### Find the thin outer shell

The rock under our feet feels hard, but Earth is not one solid stone ball. Its outer crust is thin. Beneath it are a thick mantle and a metallic core. Compare their thicknesses with one shared ruler first.

*Scene cue: Compare the common-scale cutaway with the separate shallow enlargement; do not compare their apparent thicknesses directly.*

### Solid material can slowly deform

Most of the mantle is solid. During an earthquake wave it responds as a solid, yet over an enormous span of time it can slowly deform. A tectonic plate includes crust and some uppermost mantle, rather than crust alone.

*Scene cue: Identify crust, uppermost mantle and lithosphere together in the shallow enlargement.*

### Waves bring clues from the interior

Earthquakes send out different waves. Some cross liquids; others cannot cross in their original shearing form. Records from many locations help scientists investigate the hidden interior. Rock samples provide another clue, but cannot answer every question.

*Scene cue: Switch P and S waves and receivers. Qualify shadows as principal direct-wave results, and compare mantle xenoliths with iron meteorites.*

### Zoom into the same rift

A continent can slowly stretch while rocks move across faults. Follow the same place downward. One branch shows rock storing deformation before rapid slip. Another follows mostly solid mantle material: only part melts under suitable conditions, and it can cool underground. The branches are related, but an earthquake does not shake the rocks into magma.

*Scene cue: Hold the stage at Rift A while changing whole, regional and P1-material scales. Pause geology before replaying F1 and retain its offset. Compare unloaded, no-melt, early-cessation and underground-intrusion cases. Twenty tags split into arbitrary portions rather than a measured melt fraction.*
