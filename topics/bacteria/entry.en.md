## Bacteria have cellular organisation

Bacteria are prokaryotic cells with membranes, cytoplasm, DNA and ribosomes; the nucleoid has no nuclear membrane. The illustrated peptidoglycan wall provides supporting mesh, distinct from the selective membrane. Other bacteria have an outer membrane; a few lack a wall. Membrane proteins transport particular substances, with different energy and concentration requirements. Ribosomes read messenger RNA to make proteins. Many bacteria reproduce through binary fission, copying and partitioning DNA before separating, unlike viruses assembled within a host.

## Metabolism connects chemistry with ecological roles

Selected lactic-acid bacteria ferment milk sugar and acidify it, helping proteins form yogurt gel. Other bacteria participate in decomposition, nitrogen transformations or gut metabolism. These abilities depend on particular pathways, not the label “bacterium”. Communities often exchange products with other microbes, so outcomes may arise from several organisms.

## Growth and effects depend on conditions

Nutrients, available water, temperature, pH and oxygen limit growth, with differing oxygen requirements among bacteria. Approximate doubling does not continue indefinitely as resources and wastes change. Effects on a host depend on strain, location and circumstances rather than an organism’s drawn shape or a permanent good/bad label.

## A common misconception

Bacteria are not viruses, and not all cause disease. Similar shapes do not guarantee similar abilities or effects.

## Model limits

The main cell represents one thick-walled type; colors, counts and dimensions are illustrative. Membrane exchange shows only two selected routes. Division runs once; real processes may overlap and have different timing. Habitats show different communities. These images cannot identify species or establish food safety.

### Meet a tiny cell

Does the word bacteria make you think of illness? There are many kinds, and many do not make us ill. Look at this enlarged cell. Its outer wall helps hold its shape. Just inside, a membrane selectively lets some substances enter or leave. Further in are DNA that stores information and small structures that make proteins. Press the membrane button, pause, and follow where the tiny nutrient goes.

### A change in yogurt

Switch to yogurt. Carefully selected lactic-acid bacteria use milk sugar and produce lactic acid, helping make yogurt tangy and thick. This does not mean any unknown bacterium can make suitable yogurt. Pause and notice how the tiny world connects with familiar food.

*Scene cue: Pause at the yogurt scene for three seconds.*

### Neighbours in soil and bodies

Some soil bacteria work with fungi and others to break down plant remains and recycle materials. Many bacteria also live in the gut, including kinds that process food components we cannot easily digest. Different bacteria have different abilities; no single kind necessarily performs every job.

*Scene cue: Compare soil and gut roles.*

### From one cell to two

How do little cells become more numerous? Let us watch once, slowly. The cell grows and makes a copy of its DNA. The two copies separate, and a partition forms between them. Now there are two cells! They still need suitable water, food and surroundings, so they cannot keep doubling forever. Remember, similar shapes do not mean the same abilities, and learning about these residents does not mean tasting unknown things.

*Scene cue: Play one growth-and-division sequence. Pause after DNA copying, then continue until two daughter cells form and hold the final state.*
