## One Moon, two views

The Sun lights approximately half the lunar sphere. How much of that half is visible depends on viewing geometry. Ordinary phases are not caused by Earth's shadow. The gold dot marks the near side; the space and Earth views share the lunar mesh, texture and illumination geometry.

## Model conditions

Orbit and phases use simplified geometry, not a live ephemeris. The geocentric view keeps lunar north up, enlarges the disk and boosts the dark side for visibility. It is not an Earthshine calculation or a prediction of local sky orientation. Body sizes and distances are scaled separately except in the true-scale comparison. Inclination, libration, eccentricity and eclipses are omitted; maps and clouds are static.

## Phase depends on the illuminated hemisphere we see

Moonlight is mainly reflected sunlight. Apart from eclipses and special geometry, approximately half the Moon is sunlit, while the fraction visible from Earth changes. The phase angle α is measured at the Moon between directions to Sun and observer. For an ideal sphere, the illuminated fraction of its projected disk is k=(1+cosα)/2; it is a geometric area, not total luminous output. The display holds lunar north up, so first quarter is lit on the right and last quarter on the left. The disk’s tilt relative to a real horizon also varies with location and time; this is not a universal sky orientation.

## Why two lunar periods differ

Relative to distant stars, the Moon completes an orbit in about 27.3 days, a sidereal month. New moon to new moon averages about 29.5 days, a synodic month. Earth moves around the Sun during the lunar orbit, so the Moon travels farther to restore the same Sun–Earth–Moon geometry. Individual synodic months vary somewhat around this average.

## Keeping one face toward Earth requires rotation

The Moon’s spin period nearly equals its sidereal orbital period, keeping roughly the same hemisphere toward Earth. This is synchronous rotation, not absence of spin. Seen from a fixed direction in space, the surface turns once per orbit. Libration reveals slightly more than half the surface over time. The far side also has day and night; it is not permanently dark.

## A common misconception

Ordinary phases are not Earth’s shadow. A lunar eclipse occurs when Earth blocks sunlight from reaching the Moon, and does not happen at every full moon.

## Model limits

Both views use the same surface and lighting geometry. The enlarged Earth view is a north-up geocentric approximation, with enhanced night-side visibility and no earthshine or local-horizon calculation. The circular orbit omits inclination, eccentricity, libration and eclipses. True scale compares average sizes and distance, not the current phase arrangement.

### One half in sunlight

The Moon sometimes looks round and sometimes curved. Imagine looking from space: sunlight arrives from one side and lights the half facing it. The Moon does not make its own light like the Sun. Its shape seems to change because sometimes we see more of that bright half and sometimes less.

### Follow it around

Move slowly from new Moon to a half-lit disk and then full Moon. Pause and think: has the bright half disappeared, or has our viewing angle changed? Take your time, then enlarge the Earth view and find the dark markings. The light changes, but the surface has not been replaced. Both pictures show the same Moon from different places.

### It turns too

The Moon turns as it goes around Earth, keeping roughly the same side toward us. Imagine walking around a chair while always facing it. After one lap, you have turned all the way around too. The side facing away from Earth also receives sunlight. It is not always dark.

*Scene cue: Enable the surface marker and follow its Earth-facing direction.*
