# Ice and climate: from ancient glaciations to glaciers today

There was more than one ice age. Compare evidence at different ages, then zoom the time scale. Some intervals contain others; some lie over a billion years apart.

Begin with the multiscale workbench, then continue into ancient glacial records, reflection and ocean questions. Observations can be sought backward or played explicitly over finite intervals; illustrations do not become climate reconstructions.

## From the global ice edge to A and a small sample {#ice-journey}

### Step one: how does area become a spherical ice edge?

Land outlines, relief, ocean and ice textures are original hypothetical forms. They use neither a modern basemap nor a paleogeographic reconstruction of the Marinoan or another glaciation. Select expansion or retreat, explicitly play, pause or scrub backward. A full presentation takes eighteen seconds, with ten reduced-motion stages. **Those seconds cannot be converted to actual years or edge speeds.** Full cover is a testable ideal end member, not evidence that the entire 720–635 Ma interval was identically frozen.

Area is not a linear latitude. Symmetric polar caps covering fraction f of a reference sphere obey `sin(edge latitude)=1−f`. The diagram adds a zero-mean longitude perturbation, making an irregular boundary while retaining nominal area. That perturbation is zero at A’s reference meridian. Its readout is therefore **the ice edge at A’s meridian**, not a boundary shared by all longitudes or an average latitude.

This geometry calculates no temperature, ocean heat transport or climate stability. Cover still links to one budget of 100 uniformly incident light portions, divided into reflection and absorption. Real solar angles, latitude-dependent sunlight, seasons and clouds are absent; a reflectivity slider cannot date a glaciation. Real Snowball questions also involve carbon cycling, volcanism and weathering. [Hoffman and colleagues on Cryogenian geology and climate mechanisms](https://doi.org/10.1126/sciadv.1600983)

### Step two: where do ice at A’s sea and uplands originate?

| The same hypothetical region A | Starting water source | What the gallery shows | What must not be joined |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Sea branch | Seawater | Global edge membership links to regional sea-ice context; P1 examines ice grains and brine pores | This seawater does not freeze and slide uphill to become a land glacier. |
| Upland branch | Snowfall and other accumulation | The 3D valley consumes one water-equivalent budget; P2 examines snow, firn and air spaces | A global area stage does not determine glacier mass or advance local rounds. |

Sea ice forms in seawater. Growing ice crystals exclude most salt, with some brine draining and some retained in pores and channels. Valley glaciers instead develop from snow on land through compaction, recrystallization and related processes. These are distinct sources, rather than successive transformations of one sample. [NSIDC: sea-ice formation and brine](https://nsidc.org/learn/parts-cryosphere/sea-ice/science-sea-ice), [NSIDC: snow, firn and glaciers](https://nsidc.org/learn/parts-cryosphere/glaciers/science-glaciers)

A’s sea can lie outside the assigned cover while its uplands retain glaciers; elevation, snowfall and sustained balance also matter. The local glacier still begins with sixty water-equivalent units and has twelve independent rounds. A global-stage change does not advance its rounds, and returning to the globe does not erase local progress. Playing global, local or sample observations pauses the other clocks.

The valley’s mass envelope and terminus can shrink. **A retreating terminus does not mean the whole glacier flows uphill.** Real ice can continue moving down while losses exceed supply. This gallery smooths neighboring mass-budget observations without solving velocity, stress or erosion. Its changing outline is not a material streamline.

### Step three: make sample changes explicit and read evidence in layers

P1–P5 are original teaching samples associated with A, rather than objects measured from the ideal globe. They retain a site context while having different materials and questions. Switching samples must not pretend to retain one parcel of seawater or one ice crystal.

| Sample | What can be observed? | What must still be asked? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| P1 Sea-ice section | Fixed grains and brine channels, with some brine retained after export | Structure does not measure salinity, thickness or freezing rates; salt is not all removed. |
| P2 Upland snow | Air spaces shrink and some close | P1 sea ice does not become P2 snow; closure conditions and age differences vary by site. |

| P5 Modern ice core | Layers, bubbles and different chronologies | Ice and gas ages differ; this is not a core preserving Cryogenian air. |

P1/P2 playback illustrates uncalibrated structural processes. P3/P4/P5 only remove observation cover, without replaying lithification, drilling or dating. Sorting, polish, scratches, paleomagnetism and ages constrain each other; attractive textures alone provide no diagnosis. [NPS: till and glacial flour](https://www.nps.gov/articles/glacialtillandglacialflour.htm), [NPS: glacial striations](https://www.nps.gov/articles/glacialstriations.htm)

Modern ice-core bubbles retain air when firn pores close, so air at a depth is generally younger than the surrounding ice. Comparing gas and ice events requires chronology models. These methods do not obtain Cryogenian air from surviving ancient ice. [NSIDC: what ice cores reveal](https://nsidc.org/learn/ask-scientist/core-climate-history)

### Ask further: what do the three pause buttons pause?

The global control pauses cover observations, the local one pauses a water-equivalent budget, and the sample control pauses structure or reveal. None stops nature or establishes a common seconds-to-years conversion. Changing scales or prerequisites, leaving the viewport, backgrounding and exiting pauses playback. Returning retains valid state without autoplay. Keep an intermediate stage and distinguish observation, inference and what remains unknown.

## More than one ice age

This gallery selects major events and backgrounds; it does not list every regional glaciation. Climate backgrounds, stratigraphic units, glacial events and interglacials are different levels of description.

### Huronian glaciations

**About 2.45–2.22 billion years ago; repeated glaciation**

Around early atmospheric oxygenation, Earth experienced several ancient glaciations. This was not two hundred million years of identical weather.

**Where is the evidence?** Ancient rocks, including Canada's Huronian Supergroup, preserve traces of glacial transport and deposition. Dating and correlating regions requires several kinds of evidence.

**How is it explained?** Increasing oxygen may have affected methane and other greenhouse gases, one proposed mechanism. Continental positions, volcanism and carbon cycling also matter; oxygen is not a single climate switch.

**What remains uncertain?** Regional correlations, ice extent at low latitudes, event order and triggers remain under study. This broad envelope is not an exact global start and end date.

### Sturtian glaciation

**About 717–659 million years ago**

A long Cryogenian glaciation. Glacial evidence from low latitudes helped motivate research into Snowball Earth.

**Where is the evidence?** Glacial deposits, paleomagnetic estimates of ancient latitude, dated volcanic ash and postglacial cap carbonates provide complementary clues. There is no photograph of the ancient global ice cover.

**How is it explained?** Bright ice can amplify cooling. Long-term volcanic carbon dioxide release and changes in weathering help explain eventual deglaciation.

**What remains uncertain?** Open-water areas, ice thickness and refuges for life remain research questions. The entire Cryogenian should not be shown as one unchanging sealed snowball.

### Marinoan glaciation

**About 645–635 million years ago; onset less certain**

Another widespread glaciation ended around 635 million years ago, separated from the earlier event by an interval.

**Where is the evidence?** Glacial strata and overlying cap carbonates can be correlated between continents. Dating constrains the end; low-latitude reconstructions use indirect paleomagnetic evidence.

**How is it explained?** Albedo, carbon cycling, oceans and volcanism interact. Our reflection experiment explains one feedback, not the whole deglaciation.

**What remains uncertain?** Onset and regional synchrony have uncertainty. The approximately 645-million-year boundary shown here is a research estimate.

### Late Ordovician ice and extinction

**A key phase about 445–444 million years ago**

When most complex life lived in oceans, growing continental ice, sea-level change and marine environmental changes contributed to a mass extinction.

**Where is the evidence?** Glacial deposits, sea-level records, fossil turnover and geochemistry must be considered together.

**How is it explained?** Cooling and falling sea level reduced some shallow-water habitats. Later warming and low oxygen also feature in explanations; extinction was not a single freezing switch.

**What remains uncertain?** This approximate window marks a key glacial phase; regional glaciation was more complex. Impacts of different drivers on different organisms remain distinct questions.

### Late Paleozoic icehouse

**Roughly 360–260 million years ago; repeated advances and retreats**

Coal forests, the assembly of Pangaea and southern continental glaciers belonged to an interacting Earth system.

**Where is the evidence?** Glacial deposits across Gondwana, sea-level cycles and fossil plants provide records to compare.

**How is it explained?** Plants and rock weathering influence carbon; plates change latitude and land–sea geography. Orbital rhythms can modulate shorter ice-volume variations.

**What remains uncertain?** Ice did not appear or disappear everywhere at once. Forest carbon burial was one factor, not a reason to attribute every glaciation to plants removing oxygen or carbon dioxide.

### Development of Antarctic ice sheets

**Major development from about 34 million years ago to today**

Antarctica has not always had the same ice cover. Early East and West Antarctic ice did not expand at the same time.

**Where is the evidence?** Marine cores, microfossils and oxygen isotopes, nearshore Antarctic samples and models jointly constrain ice history.

**How is it explained?** Carbon dioxide, ocean connections, topography and continental positions affect ice formation. Ice changes reflection and oceans in return.

**What remains uncertain?** Ice extent and volume changed through time. The long bar represents an icehouse background, never an unchanged ice volume lasting 34 million years.

### Quaternary glacial–interglacial cycles

**About 2.58 million years ago to today**

Large ice sheets repeatedly grew and shrank within a long-term icehouse. We live in a warmer interglacial, not on an Earth without ice.

**Where is the evidence?** Marine sediments, ice cores, moraines and glacial scratches record different time scales. Bubbles preserve past air; ice isotopes help infer environments.

**How is it explained?** Orbital changes redistribute seasonal and latitudinal sunlight, with ice, carbon dioxide and ocean feedbacks amplifying responses. Earth does not simply move ever farther from the Sun.

**What remains uncertain?** Dominant rhythms and regional responses vary. Orbital cycles cannot explain today's rapid global warming driven by increasing human-produced greenhouse gases.

### The most recent glacial maximum

**About 26,000–19,000 years ago; regional maxima differed**

North American and Eurasian ice sheets were much larger than today, but Earth was not a completely frozen snowball. Diverse tropical ecosystems remained.

**Where is the evidence?** Moraines, eroded landforms, marine sediments and sea-level indicators such as corals help reconstruct ice and coastlines.

**How is it explained?** Water stored in land ice lowered sea level. Deglaciation returned water to oceans while changing climates and habitats.

**What remains uncertain?** Maximum extent and timing differed by region. Megafaunal losses involved environmental change, humans and other factors; one worldwide cause cannot explain all species.

### Holocene: our current interglacial

**About 11,700 years ago to today**

Even in this warmer interglacial, Antarctica, Greenland and mountains retain ice. Today's rapid warming occurs on top of that background.

**Where is the evidence?** Ice cores, tree rings, sediments and corals extend history; thermometers, ocean instruments and satellites observe modern change. Each has a different resolution.

**How is it explained?** Increasing modern greenhouse gases alter the energy budget. Natural past changes do not imply that today's change has the same cause or speed.

**What remains uncertain?** The global warming trend is clear; local snowfall and individual glaciers respond to terrain, oceans and lags. One cold winter does not overturn long-term evidence.

## Ice changes sunlight

Keep sunlight on the same surface patch fixed. Change only the fraction covered by bright ice and snow, and follow the same 100 portions of sunlight.

Dark water reflects a fixed 10% here. Ice and snow can reflect 50–90%. These are chosen teaching surface values, not global mean planetary albedo.

Warming reduces snow and ice → Darker surface is exposed → More sunlight is absorbed → Warming is amplified

Positive feedback amplifies an initial change; positive does not mean beneficial. The reverse can amplify cooling as ice grows and reflection increases. Climate also radiates heat outward and responds to greenhouse gases, clouds, ocean storage and transport. This reflection budget cannot output surface temperature, freezing thresholds or the date of another glaciation.

## Ice budgets and oceans

A glacier stores water: snowfall adds to it; melt and other ablation remove it. Even a cold place can lose ice if supply is small. Hold one condition fixed and change the other.

Accumulation adds glacier mass through snowfall and other processes; ablation removes it through melting, sublimation, calving and more. Here they are reduced to two controlled quantities. Real glaciers flow downhill, and terminus positions lag climate. Losing mass does not mean ice stops moving. Persistent negative balance is more informative than one weather event.

The same hypothetical glacier starts with 60 water-equivalent units. Shape only indicates ice amount, without solving flow, slope or thickness. Rounds are not years, and loss cannot exceed available ice.

### Put equal ice mass on land or already afloat: what changes?

Equal water-equivalent masses in a freshwater approximation. Floating ice already displaced its own weight of water, so its direct melt effect is approximately zero. Land ice adds water to the ocean. Water-level changes are exaggerated and are not real meters.

Real sea level also responds to thermal expansion, salinity, currents, gravity and land motion. Density and salinity corrections make floating ice's direct effect different from exact mathematical zero. Losing floating ice shelves can also reduce restraint on land-ice flow and raise sea level indirectly. We separate two main mechanisms; sea-ice loss is not harmless.

## How evidence spans time

Moraines, scratches and transported boulders show that ice passed a place; one scratch cannot determine global ice volume. Oxygen isotopes in marine microfossils respond to water temperature, ice volume and other factors, requiring independent constraints. Air bubbles and surrounding ice need not have identical ages because pore closure takes time. Tree rings, corals and layered sediments add recent records; dating errors and differing resolutions matter. Modern instruments and satellites extend observation, but a short instrumental record is not a complete billion-year curve.

Air in a bubble is generally younger than the enclosing ice at the same depth. Comparing gas changes with ice-isotope changes requires accounting for this gas–ice age difference.

## A common misconception

Cryogenian glaciations, Quaternary cycles, the most recent glacial maximum and the Holocene are not one event. Interglacials retain continental ice. Positive feedback does not mean beneficial. Floating ice has approximately no direct sea-level effect, but its loss still matters. Past natural change does not make orbital cycles the cause of rapid modern warming.
