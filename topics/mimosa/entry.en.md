Can plants without muscles close quickly and bend slowly?

Try three stations: local mimosa touch and the primary pulvinus; one, two close and two spaced flytrap touches; then left, right and two-sided light for a seedling. Changing view retains progress; changing conditions starts a new trial. Compare turgor change, rapid leaf deformation and differential growth.

## Different organs move at different levels

Pulvini occur at leaflet, pinna and petiole bases. Local touch can trigger sequential upward leaflet folding along the touched pinna; a wider response may also reach the primary pulvinus and lower the petiole. This longitudinal section specifically shows the primary pulvinus, so it stays still during the local-touch example. Plants coordinate responses through membrane voltage and calcium signals, but have neither neurons nor a nervous system. Electrical signalling does not imply animal nerve transmission.

## Why does one touch usually not close it?

Experiments show a calcium signal spreading and then declining after a trigger hair is stimulated. A further stimulus soon afterwards can reach the closure condition. The rapid phase involves curvature change and elastic instability; it is not a copy of a mimosa pulvinus. This example uses an approximately 30-second integration window, not a universal hard threshold. One slow hair deflection may itself produce multiple signals. Signal bars and curves are qualitative, closure is slowed down, and digestion, actual prey capture and reopening over hours or longer are omitted. New trial is replay, not instant biological recovery.

## Is it turning, or growing?

In shoot phototropism, blue-light sensing and asymmetric auxin transport and response help regulate cell elongation. Greater elongation on the shaded side gradually bends the shoot toward the light. Roots respond differently to auxin and environmental conditions; this picture does not describe every organ.

## A common misconception

No muscles does not mean no movement, and the plants do not all use the same mechanism. Short-lived flytrap signals are not human thinking; light does not pull a growing shoot.

## Model limits

These independent teaching experiments have no shared real-time clock. Mimosa recovery is compressed, flytrap closure slowed down and seedling growth shown in time-lapse. Geometry, calcium signals and growth are qualitative, not micrographs or measurements. The approximately 30-second flytrap window is not universal; one slow hair deflection may produce multiple signals. Replay and reset are not instant biological recovery. Avoid repeatedly stimulating real plants.

### Plants move too

Plants have no muscles like ours and do not pick up their pots and walk. Yet some leaves close quickly, while young shoots slowly bend as they grow. Explore three movements: first predict what will trigger each one, then look at how the plant does it.

*Scene cue: Point out the three stations and begin with open mimosa leaves.*

### Mimosa changes support

Touch the mimosa in the picture. Its leaflets do not all close at the same instant. The touch produces signals; ions and water redistribute in the pulvini, changing turgor and posture. Explore the main pulvinus and the same motor cell, separating tiny leaflet folding from petiole drooping. Given time, the plant can recover.

*Scene cue: Try local touch, then the primary pulvinus and motor cell.*

### The flytrap awaits more signals

Find the fine trigger hairs inside the Venus flytrap. In this experiment one brief touch leaves it open. Another soon afterwards lets the signals build up and the leaf snap shut. Wait a long time instead, and the first signal has faded. This is not counting in a human mind; signals and leaf tissues have their own physical processes.

*Scene cue: Compare one touch, two close touches and a forty-second interval; inspect the mechanism.*

### Fast-forward growth

Now watch a seedling. Light from the left makes the shoot gradually bend left. A new trial with right-side light bends it right. Cells on the shaded side elongate more, curving the growing shoot. The seedling lit from both sides also gets taller, without bending sideways in our comparison. Some plant movements become clear only when time passes.

*Scene cue: Run separate left- and right-light trials; enlarge the growth region and compare the two-sided control.*
